Nancy Gardner Cassels examines the legal evolution and social legislation of the East India Company in British India, highlighting the complex tension between **public justice and public instruction** as the administration attempted to reform indigenous practices. The author explores how the Company navigated a **labyrinth of conflicting legal traditions**, balancing English statute law with traditional Hindu and Muslim legal codes through the doctrine of justice, equity, and good conscience. Furthermore, the text details the transformation of **criminal jurisprudence**, noting how the British systematically replaced traditional Islamic punishments with standardized colonial penal codes while grappling with issues like corruption among native law officers. Finally, the book highlights the Company's aggressive campaigns against violent crimes such as **thagi, dacoity, and ritualized sacrifices**, demonstrating how the administration weaponized the law to consolidate its political power and establish social control.

## **Understanding Early Anglo-Indian Law: A Conceptual Primer on Legal Frameworks, Native Officers, and Terminology** {#anglo-indian-law}

![eiconhorsered](https://cdn.britannica.com/73/152473-050-F8E3E857/Official-paper-watercolour-procession-East-India-Company.jpg)

### 1. Foundations of Early Anglo-Indian Jurisprudence

#### 1.1 Introduction of English Legal Frameworks (1661–1726)

English statute law was initially introduced to the Indian subcontinent through Royal Charters issued by King Charles II in 1661 and 1668, which authorized the East India Company to exercise judicial authority within its factory settlements. However, legal historians widely regard the **Charter of 1726** as the definitive vehicle that established British statute law in India. This charter created royally-chartered **Mayor’s Courts** and **Courts of Quarter Sessions** across the three presidency towns of Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay.

Under the Letters Patent of 1726, these courts were required to follow legal procedures derived directly from English law. Civil grants of probate and testamentary jurisdiction were recognized by English courts, while criminal indictments were tried before grand and petty juries. Crucially, non-Christian Indians were initially barred from serving on these juries—a restriction that remained in force for a century until statutory reforms under _Wynn’s Jury Act [7 Geo IV c. 37 (1826)]_ opened petty juries to non-Christian Indians, followed by _[2 & 3 Wm IV c. 17 (1832)]_, which extended grand jury eligibility to non-Christian Indians.

The structural contrast between early Company courts and the royally-chartered courts established from 1726 onward is outlined below:

| Company-Authorized Courts (Pre-1726)                                                                                                                | Royally-Chartered Mayor’s Courts (1726 Onwards)                                                                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Source of Authority:** Derived secondhand through East India Company commercial charters; judicial power was subordinate to presidency governors. | **Source of Authority:** Derived directly from Royal Authority via Crown Letters Patent, establishing sovereign judicial independence from local presidency governors.                        |
| **Legal Procedures:** Impromptu, localized arbitration relying on informal native officers, caste heads, or presidency governors acting as judges.  | **Legal Procedures:** Formal English common law and statutory procedures, civil probate/testamentary jurisdiction, and criminal proceedings based on English indictment.                      |
| **Jury Composition:** No formal English jury framework in place across presidency settlements.                                                      | **Jury Composition:** Grand and petty juries established; eligibility restricted exclusively to Christians until statutory reforms in 1826 (_7 Geo IV c. 37_) and 1832 (_2 & 3 Wm IV c. 17_). |

**Synthesis:** The Charter of 1726 established judicial independence for the Mayor's Courts by grounding their authority directly in the Crown rather than the East India Company. This independence generated immediate structural friction between local presidency Governors and the courts—demonstrated by early jurisdictional clashes in Bombay (1730) over caste rulings and in Madras (1736) over religious oaths. Furthermore, it created a lasting institutional tension between fixed English statutory procedures and the uncodified, dynamic customs of the indigenous population.

#### 1.2 The Preservation of Personal Laws: Hastings' 1772 Plan & The 23rd Rule

When the East India Company assumed the _diwani_ (revenue administration) of Bengal, Governor-General Warren Hastings introduced his foundational "Plan for the Administration of Justice" in 1772. Central to this plan was the **23rd Rule**, designed to preserve indigenous personal legal traditions within Company civil courts (_Mofussil Dewannee Adawlut_).

"That in all suits regarding inheritance, marriage and caste and other religious usages or institutions, the laws of the Koran with respect to Mahomedans, and those of the Shaster with respect to Gentoos shall be invariably adhered to."

The reserved legal domains established under Hastings' plan and subsequent statutory clarifications included:

- **Inheritance:** Rules dictating how ancestral and personal property passed to legitimate heirs.
- **Succession:** Principles governing titles, landed estates, and legal representation—formally added to Hastings' list by Elijah Impey in Section 37 of _Bengal Judicial Regulation VI of 1781_ and reinforced for the Supreme Court in Calcutta under Section 17 of the imperial parliament's _Declaratory Act of 1781 (21 Geo III c. 70)_.
- **Marriage:** Sacred rites, marital rights, and familial obligations recognized by Hindu and Muslim traditions.
- **Caste:** Internal communal rules, social standing, and traditional caste disciplinary usages.
- **Religious Usages and Institutions:** Domestic customs, religious endowments (_waqfs_ and _debutter_ lands), and ritual practice.

**Synthesis:** As legal historian J.D.M. Derrett highlights, Hastings selected inheritance, marriage, caste, and religious usages because these specific domains were understood in traditional Indian jurisprudence to be rooted in "unseen motives" (_adrishta_). Because these family and domestic usages were governed by spiritual concepts, foreign interference in them carried an enormous risk of civil resistance. By leaving personal family law intact under traditional sacred texts while the East India Company assumed commercial, revenue, and secular control, Hastings sought to stabilize civil society and prevent popular revolt against foreign governance.

_Having established this reserved civil framework, British judges—who lacked training in local languages and sacred jurisprudence—were forced to rely directly on indigenous legal scholars to interpret Hindu and Islamic texts._

### 2. Native Judicial Roles and Local Legal Officers

#### 2.1 Primary Court Officers: Pandits and Kazis

To navigate the reserved subjects under Hastings' Plan, British judges attached native law officers directly to the civil and criminal courts to interpret sacred Sanskrit and Arabic texts.

```txt
                          ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
                          │   Company Court (British Judge)        │
                          └───────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                              │
                     ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
                     ▼                                                 ▼
        ┌─────────────────────────┐                       ┌─────────────────────────┐
        │      Hindu Pandit       │                       │       Muslim Kazi       │
        ├─────────────────────────┤                       ├─────────────────────────┤
        │ • Consults Dharmasastra │                       │ • Consults Koran/Sharia │
        │ • Issues Vyavastha      │                       │ • Consults Hidayah      │
        │ • Texts: Dayabhaga /    │                       │ • Issues Futwa          │
        │   Mitakshara            │                       │ • Oversees Criminal Law │
        └─────────────────────────┘                       └─────────────────────────┘
```

##### **The Pandit (Hindu Law Officer)**

- **Role & Primary Texts:** The Pandit consulted sacred texts (_Shastras_, _Smritis_, and _Dharmasastra_) to advise British judges on Hindu personal law, issuing written legal opinions termed _**vyavasthas**_.
- **Key Compilations & "Schools of Law":** To standardize these opinions, Hastings commissioned eleven pandits (1773–1775) to compile the _Vivadarnave-setu_, translated by Nathaniel Brassey Halhed as _A Code of Gentoo Laws_ (1776). Later, Sir William Jones commissioned Jagannatha Tarkapancanana to assemble a broader digest (_Vivada-bhangarnava_), translated by Henry Thomas Colebrooke. Colebrooke subsequently translated the _Dayabhaga_ (prevalent in Bengal) and the _Mitakshara_ (prevalent in Benares and western/southern India). As Ludo Rocher observed, Colebrooke's translation framing mistakenly established the idea that Hindu law was divided into rigid regional "schools of law," imposing a European textual orthodoxy onto what was historically a fluid, highly localized legal tradition.
- **The "Venal Pandits" Historiography:** British officials frequently accused Pandits of corruption ("venal Pandits"). In 1788, Sir William Jones wrote to Lord Cornwallis that he could not conscientiously concur in decisions based solely on the written opinions of native lawyers who had an interest in misleading the court. This suspicion was amplified by Francis W. Macnaghten's _Considerations on the Hindoo Law_ (1824) and William Hay Macnaghten's reports. However, late 19th-century jurists such as Justice J.B. Norton and Dwarka Nath Mitter—citing the Privy Council’s landmark ruling in the _Ramnad Case (Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramlinga Sathupathy, 1868)_—demonstrated that variations in _vyavasthas_ reflected a living, adaptable legal science adapting sacred texts to local custom, rather than simple dishonesty.

##### **The Kazi / Cazi / Maulvi (Muslim Law Officer)**

- **Role & Primary Texts:** The Kazi (or Maulvi) interpreted Islamic law (_Sharia_) for civil matters involving Muslims and across the entire criminal justice system (_Nizamut_). Their consultations relied on the Koran, _Hadith_ (tradition), _Ijma_ (consensus of scholars), and _Kiyas_ (analogical reasoning), drawing heavily on compilations of Sunni Hanafi jurisprudence such as the _**Hidayah**_ (translated by Charles Hamilton under Hastings' patronage).
- **Judicial Output:** The Kazi delivered formal legal rulings known as _**futwas**_, which outlined guilt, applicable punishments, or evidentiary bars under Islamic criminal jurisprudence.

**Synthesis:** British administrators originally viewed Hindu and Islamic legal traditions as static, text-bound systems analogous to European statutory codes. In doing so, they drastically underestimated the dynamic role of unwritten custom and regional usage in modifying sacred texts.

#### 2.2 Communal, Local, and Regional Authorities

Alongside primary court officers, several secondary and regional legal bodies operated within the presidency systems:

1. _**Panchayats**_ **&** _**Chowghulas**_ **/** _**Vereadores**_ **(Bombay):** Established as early as 1674 under Governor Gerald Aungier, caste _panchayats_ resolved internal caste disputes. The Company empowered _chowghulas_ (assistants to village headmen) and _vereadores_ (municipal officers inherited from Portuguese rule) as inferior courts to settle local communal disputes.
2. _**Adigar**_ **& Choultry Courts (Madras):** In early Madras, the _adigar_ (town governor) arbitrated local disputes according to custom at the town house (_choultry_). Reorganized in 1678, the Choultry Court exercised minor civil and criminal jurisdiction through local justices until its abolition in 1800.
3. _**Zamindari Cutcherry**_ **Courts (Bengal):** Operating under Bengal _zamindari_ rights acquired in Sutanati, Govindpur, and Calcutta, these courts handled civil, criminal, and revenue cases. As historical testimony by William Bolts records, these summary courts were known for oppressive practices—including flogging delinquents "even unto death" and allowing interested parties to sit as judges and inflict punishment in their own cause.
4. _**Vakeels**_**:** Indigenous legal pleaders or advocates who represented litigants, managed legal filings, and argued cases within Company courts.

_While civil courts relied on indigenous advisers to interpret sacred texts, criminal administration forced British rulers to navigate complex Islamic criminal doctrines._

### 3. Key Legal Terminology & Islamic Criminal Doctrines

#### 3.1 The Spectrum of Islamic Criminal Law

When Governor-General Cornwallis assumed control of the criminal administration (_Nizamut_) in 1790, Muslim criminal law was retained as the general law of the land (except in Bombay). Traditional Islamic criminal jurisprudence classified justice and punishment into four major categories:

| Term                        | Literal / Legal Meaning                                              | Application / Offence                                                                                                                                                            | Procedural Obstacles / Exceptions under Sharia                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _**Hadd**_                  | Defined, rigid punishment prescribed by sacred law ("right of God"). | Stoning or scourging for _zina_ (illicit intercourse); scourging for wine drinking; amputation for theft or highway robbery; capital punishment for highway robbery with murder. | **Strict Evidentiary Bars:** Circumstantial and written evidence barred; required direct oral testimony from eye-witnesses; non-Muslims, women, slaves, and interested parties barred from testifying; confessions required fourfold repetition before a Kazi and could be retracted at any time. Excluded if the thief and victim were related. |
| _**Kisas**_                 | Retaliation / equal punishment.                                      | Capital punishment for wilful murder (_katl-amd_); physical retaliation or mutilation for serious maiming.                                                                       | **Hanafi Weapon-Type Rule:** Wilful murder (_katl-amd_) was restricted strictly to killings committed with a deadly weapon (e.g., sword or spear). Right of prosecution belonged exclusively to the victim's heirs; parents could kill children or masters kill slaves exempt from liability to _kisas_.                                         |
| _**Diya**_                  | Blood money / financial compensation fine.                           | Substituted for _kisas_ when murder intent was unproven under Hanafi weapon rules or when victim's heirs pardoned the offender.                                                  | Prosecuting heirs possessed absolute authority to accept _diya_ or grant a full pardon, terminating state prosecution entirely and bypassing capital deterrence.                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| _**Tazir**_ / _**Acoobut**_ | Discretionary correction / punishment.                               | Applied to offences not specifically enumerated under _hadd_ or _kisas_, or where full legal proof was lacking.                                                                  | Left entirely to the judge's discretion; required to be less severe than formal _hadd_ or _kisas_.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |

**Synthesis:** Traditional Islamic criminal law prioritized **private justice**—treating violent offences as private wrongs against the victim or their heirs, who retained the absolute right to pardon offenders or accept blood money. This framework directly conflicted with the British concept of **public justice**, which viewed violent crime as an offence against the state requiring uniform public deterrence.

#### 3.2 British Judicial Interventions & Hybrid Legal Concepts

To bridge these conflicting legal principles, British administrators introduced several hybrid legal doctrines and procedural fictions:

- _**Seeasut**_ **(**_**Siyasat**_**):** The legal doctrine vesting discretionary, coercive authority in the sovereign ("Sultan") to punish crimes for the public good. The British invoked _seeasut_ to override restrictive Sharia evidentiary rules in the interest of public safety and state deterrence.
- **Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience:** A residual legal rule introduced by Chief Justice Elijah Impey in Section 60 of _Bengal Judicial Regulation VI of 1781_ (tracing back to Roman law and Natural Law concepts). It authorized judges to decide cases according to natural justice whenever Hindu, Islamic, or statutory law was silent or ambiguous.
- **Law of the Defendant:** A conflict-of-laws rule enacted in Section 17 of the _Declaratory Act of 1781 (21 Geo III c. 70)_ for the Supreme Court in Calcutta, and extended to the Bengal mofussil in 1795 (_Beng. Reg. VIII, Sec. 3_). It required civil disputes between parties of different faiths to be determined according to the law and usages of the **defendant's** religion.
- **Legal Fictions in** _**Futwas**_**:** Procedural workarounds initiated under Lord Cornwallis (Judicial Regulation of 13 April 1792; _Beng. Reg. IX 1793_). Cornwallis altered Hanafi homicide rules in Section 33 of the Judicial Regulation of 3 December 1790 by substituting the criminal's _intent_ for the nature of the instrument, citing Abu Hanifa's disciples Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani ("Mahomed"). Furthermore, when a Kazi's _futwa_ barred capital punishment due to Sharia rules (e.g., a witness being non-Muslim, or a murder heir refusing to prosecute), the court required the Kazi to deliver a second, hypothetical _futwa_ stating what punishment would apply _if_ the witness had been Muslim or _if_ the heir had prosecuted, allowing the state to execute sentence.

_These hybrid concepts and procedural workarounds gradually laid the groundwork for systematic statutory codification._

### 4. Structural Evolution: From Presidency Regulations to Codification

#### 4.1 Comparative Analysis of Early Codes

As British administrative rule expanded, presidency governments developed distinct approaches to applying local laws. The Bengal system under Lord Cornwallis contrasted sharply with the Bombay system under Mountstuart Elphinstone.

```txt
                  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                  │ Early Anglo-Indian Administrative Frameworks │
                  └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                                         │
                 ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
                 ▼                                               ▼
  ┌─────────────────────────────┐                 ┌─────────────────────────────┐
  │ Cornwallis Code (Bengal)    │                 │ Elphinstone Code (Bombay)   │
  ├─────────────────────────────┤                 ├─────────────────────────────┤
  │ • Text-based Law (Shastras/ │                 │ • Custom Primary Rule       │
  │   Koran)                    │                 │ • Self-contained Penal Code │
  │ • Modified Islamic Criminal │                 │   (Bom. Reg. XIV 1827)      │
  │   Law                       │                 │ • Explicit Law of Defendant │
  └─────────────────────────────┘                 └─────────────────────────────┘
```

| Dimension                                      | Cornwallis Code of 1793 (Bengal)                                                                                   | Elphinstone Code of 1827 (Bombay)                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Regional Focus**                             | Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa (extended later to Benares and Upper Provinces).                                         | Bombay Presidency.                                                                                                           |
| **Treatment of Native Custom vs. Textual Law** | Heavily prioritized **sacred textual law** ("Mahomedan Laws" and "Hindoo Laws") over unwritten local custom.       | Positioned **local custom and usage as the primary rule** above sacred textual law (_Bom. Reg. IV 1827, Sec. 26_).           |
| **Approach to Criminal Law**                   | Retained modified Islamic criminal law as the general law of the land, altering it through procedural regulations. | Promulgated a self-contained, statutory penal code (_Bom. Reg. XIV 1827_) with a scheduled list of offences and punishments. |
| **Stance on Law of the Defendant**             | Omitted initially in 1793; reintroduced for the mofussil in 1795 (_Beng. Reg. VIII, Sec. 3_).                      | Strongly enforced; enshrined as a core rule in civil (_Bom. Reg. IV 1827_) and criminal proceedings.                         |

**Synthesis:** The Cornwallis Code in Bengal viewed indigenous law through the lens of sacred written texts while reforming Islamic criminal law through piecemeal regulations. Conversely, Mountstuart Elphinstone’s Bombay approach placed custom and local usage as the primary rule above religious text (_Bom. Reg. IV 1827, Sec. 26_) and produced India's first self-contained statutory penal code (_Bom. Reg. XIV 1827_).

#### 4.2 The Phaseout of Native Officers and Macaulay's Codification Movement

Throughout the early nineteenth century, statutory interventions steadily reduced reliance on _futwas_ and native law officers:

```txt
  1817–1822                   1832                       1833                       1864
┌───────────┐              ┌───────────┐              ┌───────────┐              ┌───────────┐
│ Beng. Reg │              │ Beng. Reg │              │ Charter   │              │  Act XI   │
│ XVII/IV   │ ───────────> │  VI 1832  │ ───────────> │ Act 1833  │ ───────────> │  of 1864  │
│ Overrode  │              │ Dispensed │              │ Macaulay's│              │ Dismissed │
│  Futwas   │              │ w/ Futwas │              │ Law Comm. │              │ Pandits & │
└───────────┘              └───────────┘              └───────────┘              │   Kazis   │
                                                                                 └───────────┘
```

- **Bengal Regulations XVII (1817) & IV (1822):** Section 15 of _Bengal Regulation XVII of 1817_ marked a major milestone in non-sectarian public justice by rescinding the long-standing immunity of Benares Brahmins from capital punishment (originally granted in 1795 under Jonathan Duncan and Lord Cornwallis via _Beng. Reg. XVI & XXI of 1795_). Section 4 of Regulation XVII of 1817 empowered Nizamut Adawlut judges to override a Kazi's _futwa_ of acquittal, while Sections 1 and 2 of _Regulation IV of 1822_ enabled judges to override a _futwa_ of conviction.
- **Bengal Regulation VI of 1832:** Authorized criminal court judges to dispense with the _futwa_ entirely by utilizing juries or native assessors, marking the practical end of Islamic criminal law as the general law of Bengal.
- **Charter Act of 1833 & Macaulay’s Law Commission:** Created the position of Legal Member of Council (filled by Thomas Babington Macaulay) and established the First Law Commission. In a speech before Parliament, Macaulay famously articulated the drive for legal uniformity:

_"Our principle is simply—uniformity where you can have it—diversity where you must have it—but in all cases certainty."_ This movement produced Macaulay's Draft Penal Code of 1837 (enacted in 1860).

- **Act XI of 1864:** Formally abolished the offices of Hindu Pandit and Muslim Kazi/Law Officer across Company courts following the enactment of the Indian Penal Code (1860), ending official reliance on native legal advisers.

_This historical transition culminated in a fully codified, centralized Anglo-Indian legal structure._

#### 5.2 Key Takeaways for Students

- **Shift from Personal to Codified Law:** Anglo-Indian jurisprudence evolved from a piecemeal system that preserved personal religious laws under the 1772 Plan into a centralized, codified legal structure under Macaulay's Law Commission and the Post-1857 Crown administration.
- **Text vs. Custom Misunderstanding:** British administrators initially treated Hindu and Islamic laws as rigid, text-bound systems comparable to European codes. As Ludo Rocher noted, Colebrooke’s framing constructed artificial regional "schools of law" that overlooked the dynamic role of local custom—a defect Mountstuart Elphinstone corrected in the Bombay Code of 1827 by making custom the primary rule (_Bom. Reg. IV 1827, Sec. 26_).
- **Transformation of Criminal Justice:** Islamic criminal law was steadily transformed from a system centered on **private justice** (where heirs could pardon murderers or accept blood money) into an Anglo-Indian criminal framework centered on **public justice** and state deterrence.
- **Phaseout of Native Agencies:** As British judges accumulated case precedents, legal digests, and statutory codes, indigenous legal advisers (_Pandits_ and _Kazis_) were progressively bypassed (1817–1832) and ultimately abolished by statute under **Act XI of 1864**.

## **Historical Overview: The Evolution of East India Company Legislation and Codification** (1661–1860s) {#historical-overview}

### 1. Foundations of Colonial Jurisprudence: Charters, Early Courts, and Presidency Divergence (1661–1753)

The legal infrastructure of British India developed gradually from humble mercantile origins into a complex Anglo-Indian administrative state. Initial judicial authorization derived from the Royal Charters of 1661 and 1668 issued by King Charles II, which granted the East India Company (EIC) early judicial authority over its factory personnel and local dependents. However, legal historians widely regard the **Letters Patent / Charter of 1726** as the foundational landmark that formally introduced British statute law into the subcontinent.

Central to this legal evolution was an enduring administrative tension between two distinct modes of governance: **Public Justice**—the aggressive exercise of state power through criminal law and judicial coercion to maintain political order—and **Public Instruction**—the reliance on education, social persuasion, and administrative caution to modify custom without provoking civil unrest.

The 1726 Charter established royal courts—specifically **Mayor’s Courts** for civil matters and **Courts of Quarter Sessions** for criminal matters—across the three primary presidency towns of Bombay, Madras, and Calcutta:

- **Civil and Probate Jurisdiction:** The Mayor’s Courts were royally endowed entities whose civil grants of probate and testamentary decisions were recognized directly by courts in England. Appeals in substantial civil cases lay directly with the King in Council.
- **Criminal Administration:** Courts of Quarter Sessions comprised the local Governor and five senior members of the Council, who exercised the powers of English Justices of the Peace to try all criminal offenses except high treason.
- **Jury Restrictions and Statutory Reforms:** Criminal indictments depended on petty and grand juries; however, participation was strictly restricted to Christian residents. This statutory exclusion of non-Christian Indians persisted for a century, until Parliament enacted **Wynn’s Jury Act of 1826 (7 Geo IV c. 37)**, which admitted non-Christian Indians to petty juries, followed by the **1832 Act (2 & 3 Wm IV c. 17)**, which admitted them to grand juries.
- **Presidency Governance:** The 1726 Charter bestowed formal legislative authority upon the Governor and Council of each presidency, subject to confirmation by the EIC Court of Directors and the constraint that local regulations could not contradict English law.

Because these early courts functioned in distinct geopolitical environments, each presidency developed its own institutional approach toward indigenous populations:

#### Early Judicial Systems Across Presidencies (Pre-1772)

#### Tensions Between English Procedure and Indigenous Customs

The introduction of royal legal institutions created friction between English statutory procedures and local religious practices. A major point of contention was the administration of judicial oaths. In 1730, the EIC Court of Directors was forced to intervene in Bombay to uphold the Mayor’s Court’s independence against the Governor’s Council over religious and caste disputes. A more severe conflict erupted in Madras in 1736, when the Mayor's Court imprisoned two Hindu merchants for refusing to take judicial oaths that violated their religious rules and caste traditions. The imprisonment provoked widespread civil unrest among Hindu inhabitants. Governor George Morton Pitt eventually released the merchants on parole, prompting the Company to issue instructions upholding the court's judicial standing while cautioning against rigid enforcement that disregarded local sensitivities.

To resolve these recurring jurisdictional conflicts, the **Charter of 1753** explicitly exempted civil suits between Indian parties from the jurisdiction of the Mayor’s Courts, unless both parties voluntarily submitted the dispute to the court's judgment. While this exemption was largely ignored in Bombay and limited by the weak authority of Choultry Courts in Madras, it operated effectively in Bengal because the local Zamindari Courts already managed indigenous civil litigation.

_The operational limitations and fragmented authority of these early presidency courts ultimately demonstrated the need for a uniform legal structure, prompting Warren Hastings to initiate a comprehensive reorganization of judicial administration in 1772._

### 2. The Dual System, Reserved Personal Laws, and the Orientalist Legal Project (1772–1832)

When the East India Company assumed the _diwani_ (revenue administration) of Bengal, Warren Hastings introduced his **Plan for the Administration of Justice (1772)**. Central to this plan was the historic **23rd Rule**, which established a structural division between personal law and general administration:

> _"That in all suits regarding inheritance, marriage and caste and other religious usages or institutions, the laws of the Koran with respect to Mahomedans, and those of the Shaster with respect to Gentoos shall be invariably adhered to."_

This reservation of personal law was systematically incorporated into imperial statutory frameworks over the following decade:

- **Bengal Judicial Regulation XXVII of 1780 (Sec. 27):** Reenacted Hastings' 23rd Rule word-for-word into formal colonial regulation.
- **Bengal Judicial Regulation VI of 1781:** Chief Justice Sir Elijah Impey added the word **"succession"** to the list of reserved personal subjects (Sec. 37). Impey also introduced the fundamental residual rule in Section 60: where no specific statutory direction existed, judges were instructed to act according to **"justice, equity, and good conscience."**
- **Declaratory Act of 1781 (21 Geo III c. 70, Secs. 17 & 18):** Parliament entrenched personal law within the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court of Calcutta. Section 17 mandated that civil disputes involving native residents be determined by the **law of the defendant** in matters of inheritance, succession, and contract. Section 18 protected customary authority, decreeing that actions performed under caste rules were exempt from criminal prosecution under English standards.
- **Cornwallis Code (Beng. Reg. IV 1793, Sec. 15):** Re-articulated the reserved topics, changing the terminology from religious texts ("Koran" and "Shaster") to general categories ("Mahomedan laws" and "Hindoo Laws"). This principle was extended to Benares via **Bengal Regulation VIII of 1795 (Sec. 3)**, which integrated the law of the defendant.
- **Bombay Civil Precedents (Bom. Reg. IV 1799, Sec. 14):** Preceding the later Elphinstone Code, Bombay established that civil suits were to be decided by the law of the defendant for Hindus and Muslims, while Portuguese and Parsee cases were guided by equity and custom. Crucially, Section 14 prioritized general district usage and family custom above textual doctrines in all disputes regarding succession to landed property.

#### The "Venal Pandits" Debate and Textual Patronage

Because European judges lacked training in Sanskrit and Arabic legal traditions, early EIC courts relied on court-appointed law officers—_pandits_ for Hindu law and _maulvis_ or _kazis_ for Islamic law—to issue legal opinions (_vyavasthas_ and _futwas_). As British courts began applying English principles of precedent (_stare decisis_), discrepancies and inconsistent rulings from these native law officers caused frustration among British officials.

This dissatisfaction sparked accusations of corruption and venality against the law officers, famously voiced by Chief Justice Sir William Jones in 1788 and later amplified by legal commentators Francis W. Macnaghten and William Hay Macnaghten. To reduce reliance on oral opinions, the EIC funded an extensive text-translation project to create accessible legal manuals:

1. **Halhed’s Gentoo Code (1775):** Compiled by a committee of eleven _pandits_ commissioned by Hastings to draft the _Vivadarnave-setu_ ("Bridge Across the Ocean of Litigation"), translated into English by Nathaniel Brassey Halhed via a Persian translation.
2. **Hamilton’s Hidayah (1791):** Charles Hamilton’s translation of the primary compendium of Sunni Hanafi law.
3. **Sir William Jones’s Digest:** A proposed comprehensive digest of Hindu and Islamic law on inheritance and contract, leading to his translation of the _Manusmrti_ (_The Laws of Manu_).
4. **Jagannatha’s Vivada-bhangarnava:** The compiled digest envisioned by Jones, constructed by Jagannatha Tarkapancanana using classical logic (_mimamsa_ and _nyaya_).
5. **Colebrooke’s Two Treatises (1810):** Henry Thomas Colebrooke translated select legal commentaries, solidifying the division of Hindu law into two primary textual traditions: the **Dayabhaga** (dominant in Bengal) and the **Mitakshara** (dominant across the rest of India).

#### Major Misconceptions of British Codification

Modern legal historians have highlighted three critical structural errors in the British attempt to codify Hindu personal law:

- **Mistaking** _**Smriti**_ **for Rigid Statutory Codes:** British Orientalists treated textual Sanskrit legal treatises (_smritis_) as absolute legislative codes, overlooking the reality that classical Hindu law was fundamentally dynamic and rooted in localized custom.
- **Reduction of Dynamic Custom to Fixed "Schools of Law":** By elevating Colebrooke’s translations of the _Dayabhaga_ and _Mitakshara_ as absolute precedents, EIC courts reduced fluid regional legal customs into rigid "schools of law," restricting judicial flexibility for generations.
- **Systematic Distrust and Banishment of Native Law Officers:** British administrators attributed conflicting textual interpretations to moral corruption among the _pandits_ and _maulvis_. This institutional distrust led to their systematic marginalization, culminating in their complete statutory dismissal under **Act XI of 1864**.

- **Bengal Presidency (Beng. Reg. VII 1832):**
  - Explicitly rescinded the law of the defendant.
  - Applied **"Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience"** as the primary residual rule to prevent biased religious law from superseding natural equity.
- **Bombay Presidency (Elphinstone Code / Bom. Reg. IV 1827):**
  - **Hierarchical Civil Order:**
    1. District & Family Custom (Primary Rule)
    2. Law of the Defendant (Secondary Rule)
    3. Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience (Tertiary Resort)

While Bengal shifted away from personal choice toward centralized equity principles, Bombay established a hierarchical model that prioritized documented local usage over textual religious laws:

### 3. Transformation of Criminal Law: Public Justice, Legal Fictions, and Social Control (1790–1850s)

When Cornwallis assumed responsibility for the criminal courts (_Nizamut_) in 1790, Islamic criminal law was declared the general law of the land across Bengal and Madras (whereas Bombay reserved Hindu criminal law for Hindu litigation). Over the next seven decades, EIC administrators modified Islamic penal concepts using the doctrine of **siyasat** (_seeasut_)—the discretionary power of the sovereign ruler to enforce administrative and penal measures for the public good.

#### The 4 Core Islamic Law Punishments & EIC Statutory Reforms

- **1. Hadd (Defined Punishment):** Fixed penalties prescribed for specific offenses against divine law (e.g., stoning or scourging for illicit intercourse (_zina_); limb amputation for theft and highway robbery).
  - _EIC Modification:_ Bypassed in practice. Strict Hanafi evidentiary rules—such as requiring four eyewitnesses or rejecting circumstantial evidence, non-Muslim testimony, and written documentation—made convictions under _Hadd_ rare.
- **2. Kisas (Retaliation):** Private justice granting victims or their surviving heirs the right to exact physical retaliation (death for wilful murder, maiming for bodily injury).
  - _EIC Modification:_ Stripped next-of-kin of their right to pardon murderers or commute sentences (Beng. Reg. IX 1793). The prosecution of homicide was transformed from a private right into a public offense prosecuted exclusively by the state under **Public Justice**.
- **3. Diya (Blood Money):** Monetary compensation paid to victims or heirs in place of _Kisas_, particularly when homicide lacked explicit intent under Hanafi rules.
  - _EIC Modification:_ Abolished blood-money commutations. In the Regulation of December 3, 1790 (codified in Beng. Reg. IX 1793), Cornwallis substituted criminal intention for the physical nature of the weapon as the criterion for murder, explicitly citing Abu Hanafa’s disciples "Yusuf and Mahomed" (identified in modern scholarship as al-Shaybani).
- **4. Tazir / Seeasut (Discretionary Punishment):** Penalties imposed at the discretion of the judge or ruler for offenses not explicitly covered by _Hadd_ or _Kisas_.
  - _EIC Modification:_ Utilized as the constitutional basis for EIC legal intervention. EIC administrators expanded _seeasut_ to introduce imprisonment, hard labour, banishment, and transportation for public order offenses.

#### Socio-Economic Roots of Rural Dacoity

In analyzing the surge of gang robbery (_dacoity_) during the early nineteenth century, legal historian John R. McLane demonstrates that dacoity was not merely a policing failure, but a direct socio-economic byproduct of EIC structural reforms. The Cornwallis land revenue reforms (1793 Permanent Settlement) and the aggressive resumption of service lands (_paikan_) displaced traditional village watchmen (_chaukidars_). Stripped of their hereditary lands and livelihoods, many _chaukidars_ turned to gang robbery as _sirdars_ (dacoit leaders). Simultaneously, the Permanent Settlement eroded the traditional paternalist authority of old _zamindars_, replacing them with urban revenue farmers and competitive landholders. Facing rigid EIC revenue demands, these new landholders actively colluded with dacoit gangs, offering them sanctuary in exchange for a share of the plunder.

#### Procedural Innovations and Legal Fictions

To reform Islamic criminal law without directly invalidating its authority, the EIC employed **legal fictions**. Bengal Regulation IX of 1793 codified earlier judicial directives (1790–1792):

- **Bypassing Futwas:** If a law officer (_maulvi_) acquitted a prisoner due to procedural rules (such as the exclusion of non-Muslim witness testimony), the magistrate was instructed to demand a hypothetical second _futwa_. This second opinion declared what the verdict would be if the witness had been a Muslim or if the heir had chosen to prosecute.
- **Overriding Law Officers:** Bengal Regulation XVII of 1817 empowered the _Nizamut Adawlut_ to override _futwas_ of acquittal. Regulation IV of 1822 enabled judges to override _futwas_ of conviction. Bengal Regulation VI of 1832 permitted judges to dispense with _futwas_ entirely by utilizing non-Muslim juries or assessors, ending the operational dominance of Islamic criminal law in Bengal (a measure extended to Madras via Act I of 1840).
- **Abolition of Special Immunity:** Bengal Regulation XVII of 1817 repealed the historical exemption from capital punishment previously granted to Benares Brahmins under the 1795 regulations, establishing statutory equality in capital sentencing.

#### Anti-Gang Campaigns and Tactical Innovations

To suppress gang robbery (_dacoity_) and specialized highway murder (_thagi_), the EIC created a parallel legal apparatus that bypassed standard criminal procedures:

**Anti-Gang Legislative Evolution (1803–1848)**

- **Beng. Reg. LIII 1803:** Defined "Robbery by Open Violence" (_Suruca-i-kubra_ / _Shubkhoone_).
- **Beng. Reg. IX 1808:** Authorized the proclamation and outlawry of Dacoit Sirdars.
- **Act XXX 1836:** Criminalized membership in _Thagi_ gangs; dispensed with _futwas_.
- **Act XIX 1837:** Codified Macaulay's Approver System (validated accomplice evidence).
- **Act XXIV 1843:** Extended summary _Thagi_ rules directly to _Dacoity_ gangs.
- **Act XI 1848:** Extended summary rules to "Wandering Gangs."

The operational procedures of the **Thagi and Dacoity Department** (established under William Sleeman) relied on four primary tactical innovations:

1. **The Approver System:** Promulgated via Macaulay’s **Act XIX of 1837**, this system offered conditional pardons to captured gang members (sparing them from execution or transportation) in exchange for detailed confessions and testimony against their accomplices.
2. **Elimination of** _**Futwas**_**:** **Act XXX of 1836** removed the requirement for law officers' _futwas_ in _thagi_ trials, freeing proceedings from traditional Hanafi rules of evidence.
3. **Multi-Jurisdictional Trial Authority:** **Act XVIII of 1837** and **Act XVIII of 1843** empowered special officers to arrest, try, and detain suspects across different districts and princely states, regardless of where the specific crime occurred.
4. **Incarceration Based on Association:** Under Act XXX of 1836 and **Act XXIV of 1843**, proof of membership in a recognized gang of _thugs_ or _dacoits_ was made punishable by life imprisonment with hard labour, removing the requirement to prove participation in a specific murder or robbery.

#### Sorcery, Superstition, and Legislative Limits

The EIC’s approach to social customs and violent superstitions balanced criminal intervention (**Public Justice**) with legislative restraint (**Public Instruction**):

- **Witch-Killings and Ramgurh Trials by Ordeal:** In 1792, in the Ramgurh district, three Sutar men murdered five women declared to be witches following three distinct trials by ordeal detailed by Sir John Shore:
  1. _Saul Tree Branch Trial:_ Branches of the Saul tree marked with the names of all village women over age 12 were planted in water for four and a half hours; those whose branches withered were declared witches.
  2. _White Ant Nest Trial:_ Bags of rice marked with women's names were placed in white ant nests; if ants consumed the rice, the named woman was condemned.
  3. _Oil and Shadow Ceremony:_ Leaf-cups containing water, mustard seed, and oil were observed by lamp light while pronouncing names; any woman whose shadow appeared on the water was condemned. In response, the government issued a proclamation codified in **Bengal Regulation IV of 1797 (Sec. VI)**, declaring that killing any person on allegations of sorcery constituted murder. This rule was extended to Madras and Bombay, ultimately appearing in the **Elphinstone Code (Bom. Reg. XIV 1827, Cl. 2 Sec. XXVI)**.
- **Rejection of Sorcery Bans:** In 1837, the Law Commission rejected proposals from Madras judges to outlaw the practice of sorcery itself. Macaulay argued that criminalizing sorcery would cause the public to believe the state officially recognized magical powers.
- **Rejection of Ornament Regulations:** The Law Commission similarly rejected proposals to prohibit parents from decorating children with gold and silver ornaments to prevent murders for gain. Citing utilitarian principles, the Commission noted that parental ornamenting stimulated industry and trade, while a prohibition would prove unpopular and invite police abuse.

_The growth of piecemeal regulations, special anti-gang acts, and conflicting presidency codes created an increasingly complex legal system, generating an urgent demand for a single, comprehensive penal code across British India._

### 4. The Benthamite Era, Codification Debates, and Post-1857 Enactments (1833–1860s)

Formal codification began with the **Elphinstone Code of 1827 (Bom. Reg. XIV 1827)**, which digested Bombay’s criminal regulations into a systematic penal body. However, modern all-India codification was driven by Utilitarian ideals, particularly the philosophy of Jeremy Bentham, as advocated by James Mill and Thomas Babington Macaulay.

The codification movement was specifically catalyzed by two structural milestones:

1. **Charles Metcalfe’s 1829 Minute:** Raised the alarm regarding unedifying jurisdictional clashes between the King's Supreme Courts (applying English law) and the EIC's _Mofussil_ Courts (_Adawluts_), demanding unified legislative control.
2. **James Mill’s Despatch of December 10, 1834:** Formally framed the codification mandate of Section 53 of the **Charter Act of 1833 (3 & 4 Will IV c. 85)**.

The Charter Act of 1833 created the office of **Legal Member** of the Governor-General’s Council (first occupied by T.B. Macaulay), established the **First Law Commission**, and issued Section 53's mandate, framed by Macaulay's guiding principle:

_"Uniformity where you can have it—diversity where you must have it—but in all cases certainty."_

Macaulay completed his landmark **Draft Penal Code in 1837**. However, statutory codification faced delays due to administrative setbacks, regional opposition from Bombay judges, and fear of religious backlash.

  - _1855 Second Law Commission Report:_ Ruled against codifying religious personal laws as statutory law.
- **Substantive Civil Interventions:**
  - _1850 Caste Disabilities Removal Act (Act XXI):_ Protected property/inheritance rights of religious converts.

#### Retrenchments vs. Substantive Interventions

1. **Abandonment of the Lex Loci Proposal (1840):** The Law Commission initially suggested declaring English law the general law (_lex loci_) of British India, arguing that territory outside Hindu and Muslim personal law should be treated as an uninhabited colony. This unhistorical view was abandoned in favor of an Anglo-Indian code derived from regional precedent and equity.
2. **Exclusion of Personal Law (1855):** The Second Law Commission's 1855 Report ruled against enacting Hindu or Muslim religious laws as statutory codes, establishing that personal religious laws should remain uncodified.
3. **Targeted Civil Enactments:** The EIC selectively altered personal law through targeted civil legislation:
   - **Caste Disabilities Removal Act of 1850 (Act XXI):** Enacted clauses from the draft Lex Loci proposal, protecting the property and inheritance rights of individuals who converted from Hinduism or Islam.
   - **Hindu Widow Remarriage Act of 1856 (Act XV):** Granted statutory recognition to the remarriage of Hindu widows, altering traditional family property arrangements.

#### Critical Factors Forcing Post-1857 Passage

Between 1859 and 1864, three main factors led to the passage of the legal codes:

- **The Catalyst of the 1857 Uprising:** The political crisis of 1857 exposed the weaknesses of the East India Company's dual judicial administration. Transferring governance to the British Crown required a clear, consolidated legal framework to maintain state security.
- **Expanded Legislative Machinery:** The **Charter Act of 1853** enlarged the Governor-General’s Legislative Council to include full-time judges. Under Lord Dalhousie, this council functioned as an active legislative body capable of processing complex statutory codes.
- **The Second and Third Law Commissions:** Based in London, these commissions abandoned efforts to codify religious personal laws. Instead, they focused on procedural and criminal codification, producing the **Code of Civil Procedure (1859)**, the **Indian Penal Code (1860)**, and the **Code of Criminal Procedure (1861)**.

## **Comparative Policy Analysis Report: Statutory Frameworks, Gang Criminalization, and Procedural Innovations Governing Dacoity and Thagi in Company India** {#india-governance}

### 1. Jurisprudential Foundations and the Transformation of Criminal Law

#### 1.1 The Shift from Hanafi Criminal Law to Public Justice (_Siyasat_)

In 1790, the East India Company made a decisive institutional pivot by assuming direct responsibility for the administration of criminal justice (_Nizamut_) across its expanding Indian territories. Governor-General Lord Cornwallis declared the imperative to intervene after observing widespread operational breakdowns, procedural delay, and systemic corruption within the indigenous _Foujdary_ (criminal court) system. Prior to this intervention, criminal jurisprudence in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa was grounded in the Hanafi school of Islamic law, administered under the nominal authority of the Nazim (Mughal provincial governor) and his legal officers (_maulvis_ and _kazis_).

For the Company, displacing traditional Islamic legal structures was not merely a matter of judicial reform, but a strategic imperative. To establish sovereign authority, protect commercial corridors, collect land revenue, and suppress collective violence, the Company needed to replace a legal framework centered on private prosecution and financial restitution with a state-directed mechanism of public justice.

The primary operational barrier posed by traditional Hanafi criminal law was its foundational division of offenses into private injuries and public transgressions, placing paramount emphasis on victim-directed restitution. Offenses were categorized under four main doctrines:

1. _**Hadd**_**:** Fixed, unalterable punishments prescribed by sacred law for specific transgressions against public order (such as amputation for theft or highway robbery, and stoning or scourging for illicit intercourse). _Hadd_ was bounded by exceptionally strict procedural requirements. Convictions necessitated direct eyewitness testimony from adult Muslim males of unimpeachable character, or an uncoerced confession repeated four times before a _Kazi_ (which could be retracted at any moment prior to execution). Circumstantial, written, female, and non-Muslim evidence was strictly excluded. Furthermore, _hadd_ could not be executed if the offender and victim were related, or if the value of stolen property fell below ten _dirms_ (roughly three sicca rupees).
2. _**Kisas**_**:** Retaliation in kind (life for life, limb for limb) applied to willful murder (_katl-amd_) and severe physical maiming. _Kisas_ operated purely as a private right vested in the victim or the victim's legal heirs (_wali_). The heirs possessed absolute legal discretion to prosecute, commute the capital sentence to financial compensation (_diya_), or pardon the murderer entirely. If the heirs opted not to prosecute or accepted a pardon, the state possessed no independent standing to intervene. Crucially, hierarchical social relationships barred retaliation altogether: masters could murder their slaves, and parents or grandparents their children or grandchildren, with total legal impunity because _kisas_ could not be claimed across these patriarchal lines.
3. _**Diya**_**:** Blood-money paid as financial compensation to the victim's heirs when _kisas_ was commuted or when a killing was deemed unintentional. Traditional Hanafi doctrine, as formulated by Abu Hanafa, determined criminal intent (_katl-amd_) based strictly on the physical nature of the instrument used in the assault. Abu Hanafa maintained that intentional murder required the use of a lethal weapon made of iron, or an instrument inherently capable of causing death (such as a sword or spear). If a murder was committed using a wooden club, stone, rope, or water, Abu Hanafa classified the act as quasi-deliberate homicide (_katl-shibh-amd_), barring capital punishment (_kisas_) and restricting the remedy to _diya_.
4. _**Tazir**_ **(alongside** _**Acoobut**_ **and** _**Siyasat**_**):** Discretionary punishment inflicted by the judge or ruler for offenses where _hadd_ or _kisas_ was barred by procedural technicalities or where specific penalties were not explicitly defined by sacred texts.

To overcome these structural limitations without overtly repudiating the Islamic legal framework prior to the post-1857 transition to the Crown, the Company leveraged the Islamic legal doctrine of _siyasat_ (_seeasut_). In Hanafi jurisprudence, _siyasat_ represented the discretionary, sovereign authority vested in the ruler (_Sultan_) to enact coercive measures and administrative punishments for the preservation of public order, state security, and the public good.

Colonial administrators deployed _siyasat_ as an ideological leverage point to justify statutory overrides of traditional Hanafi rules. Citing _siyasat_, Governor-General Cornwallis explicitly bypassed Abu Hanafa's strict doctrine on lethal instruments. In Section 33 of the Judicial Regulation of December 3, 1790 (subsequently codified as Section 75 of **Bengal Regulation IX 1793**), Cornwallis overrode Abu Hanafa’s rule by embracing the alternative doctrines of his principal disciples, **Abu Yusuf** and **Muhammad al-Shaybani** (_al-Saybani_). These disciples maintained that criminal intent must be inferred from the nature of the act itself rather than the physical composition of the weapon.

Through this legislative maneuver, the Company systematically converted private prosecutions into state-directed trials, stripping heirs of pardon rights and replacing private restitution with exemplary penalties—such as life imprisonment and transportation beyond the seas—designed to uphold public justice and consolidate state power.

| Metric                   | Traditional Islamic Criminal Principles                                                                             | Anglo-Indian Statutory Reforms                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Primary Objective**    | Private restitution; satisfaction of victim/heirs (_Kisas_/_Diya_).                                                 | Preservation of state power, public order, and state-directed public justice (_Siyasat_).                                      |
| **Evidentiary Standard** | Strict oral requirements; exclusion of non-Muslim, female, and interested testimony; 4x confession before a _Kazi_. | Admissibility of circumstantial proof; universal witness competency regardless of religion (Beng. Reg. IX 1793; Act XIX 1837). |
| **Victim/Heir Role**     | Absolute private control over prosecution, pardon, and commutation to blood-money (_Diya_).                         | Elimination of heir pardon rights; mandatory state prosecution regardless of family consent (Beng. Reg. IX 1793).              |
| **State Authority**      | Auxiliary; restricted to discretionary _Tazir_ when private claims failed or were barred.                           | Supreme; executive override of Hanafi exceptions via legal fictions, statutory codes, and _siyasat_.                           |

This systematic transformation from victim-controlled private restitution to executive-managed public justice established the overarching legal rationale for colonial statutory intervention, though its institutional application diverged significantly across the three Presidencies.

#### 1.2 Inter-Presidency Jurisprudential Divergence (Bengal, Bombay, and Madras)

The development of Anglo-Indian jurisprudence was marked by deep regional variations resulting from distinct charter histories, local enactments, and competing administrative methodologies across Bengal, Bombay, and Madras. Central to this divergence was the legal distinction between the **Crown Supreme Courts** and the **Company Mofussil Courts**.

The Crown Supreme Courts (established at Calcutta in 1774, Madras in 1800, and Bombay in 1823 under English parliamentary charters such as 21 Geo. III c. 70) operated within the presidency capital towns. They applied English statutory and common law as their residual law, exercising jurisdiction over European subjects and Indian residents within town limits.

Conversely, the Company Mofussil Courts (_Nizamut Adawlut_ in Bengal, _Foujdary Adawlut_ in Madras and Bombay) exercised jurisdiction over the agrarian interior (the _mofussil_). These courts operated under modified Islamic criminal law and regional presidency regulations, creating three distinct legal architectures across Company India.

##### Bengal Presidency

Bengal functioned as the principal legal laboratory for statutory experimentation. Following the acquisition of the _Diwani_ in 1765 and the assumption of the _Nizamut_ in 1790, Bengal constructed a system that retained the external machinery of Islamic criminal law while systematically amending its substantive provisions through executive regulations. Under the Cornwallis Code of 1793 (specifically **Bengal Regulation IX 1793**), the _Nizamut Adawlut_ supervised circuit courts operating under Islamic criminal law, continuously overriding Hanafi rules on intent, evidence, and sentencing via regulatory enactments.

##### Bombay Presidency

Bombay pursued a radically different jurisprudential path. Tracing back to Governor Gerald Aungier's administration in 1674, Bombay built a legal tradition grounded in respect for local custom, caste usages, and village assemblies (_panchayats_). Bombay never recognized Islamic criminal law as the universal law of the land for non-Muslims. Instead, Hindu criminal law was reserved for Hindu subjects, and local custom was accorded explicit statutory priority.

Under Mountstuart Elphinstone’s governorship, this tradition culminated in the **Elphinstone Code of 1827** (specifically **Bombay Regulation XIV of 1827**). The Elphinstone Code created a self-contained, statutory penal code that classified offenses and apportioned punishments without relying on Islamic law or legal fictions. **Bombay Regulation IV of 1827** established custom as the primary rule of decision in civil and criminal matters. In the absence of specific statutory provisions or district usages, judicial decisions defaulted to the "law of the defendant" (_lex loci_).

##### Madras Presidency

Madras adopted a middle course, adapting Bengal’s regulatory model to its local administrative landscape. Through **Madras Regulations VII and XV of 1802**, Madras duplicated the criminal law revisions set forth in Bengal Regulation IX of 1793 and Regulation LIII of 1803. The Madras _Foujdary Adawlut_ supervised circuit courts applying modified Islamic law. However, Madras continuously struggled with institutional friction when enforcing Bengal-style regulatory codification across regions with distinct social structures and agrarian traditions, such as the Malabar coast and the Northern Circars.

##### Accommodating Non-European and Non-Muslim Communities Across Presidency Frameworks

- **Parsees:** In Bombay, **Bombay Regulation IV of 1799** explicitly commanded judges to resolve civil suits involving Parsee defendants according to principles of equity and natural justice, making full allowance for their distinct community usages, inheritance customs, and punchayet adjudications.
- **Portuguese Residents:** Inherited from the Portuguese colonial footprint in Bombay, Portuguese inhabitants were accommodated through _vereadores_ (indigenous municipal officers) and custom-focused assessments under Bombay Regulation IV of 1799, insulating them from both English statutory codes and Islamic legal doctrines.
- **Tribal and Frontier Groups:** In Bengal’s hill tracts (such as Ramgurh and Chota Nagpur) and Madras’s agency tracts (such as the Gudem and Rampa hills), regular presidency regulations were frequently suspended. Executive officers operated under political commissions, applying summary discretionary justice to manage indigenous tribal populations outside standard judicial frameworks.
- **Non-Christian Indians in Supreme Courts:** Under the East India Company Act 1781 (21 Geo. III c. 70, Section 17), the Crown Supreme Court at Calcutta preserved personal legal autonomy for Indian inhabitants in matters of inheritance, succession, and contract, restricting decisions to Hindu or Muslim law. Where parties belonged to different religious traditions, Section 17 mandated the application of the "law of the defendant." Furthermore, Section 18 protected caste usages and family acts from being prosecuted as crimes under English common law.

These regional administrative variations highlighted the urgent need for standardized procedural workarounds that could alter criminal procedure without provoking widespread indigenous resistance.

#### 1.3 Judicial Fictions and Procedural Workarounds

To modify Islamic criminal procedure without formally repudiating the Mughal legal mantle prior to the post-1857 Crown transition, the East India Company constructed deliberate legal fictions. These procedural mechanisms enabled European judges to secure convictions while maintaining the administrative appearance of continuity within the indigenous legal order.

The systematic implementation of judicial fictions began under Lord Cornwallis:

- **Substitution of Criminal Intent (1790–1793):** Citing the alternative Hanafi doctrines of Abu Yusuf and Muhammad al-Shaybani, Section 33 of the Regulation of December 3, 1790 (codified as Section 75 of **Bengal Regulation IX 1793**) commanded that the intention of the accused—as inferred from the nature of the act—be substituted for the physical instrument of death as the sole legal criterion for murder.
- **Abolition of Heir Pardons (1790–1793):** Section 34 of the December 1790 Regulation (and Section 76 of Bengal Regulation IX 1793) stripped the victim's heirs of their legal right to pardon a murderer or commute a capital sentence to _diya_, transferring absolute prosecution authority to the state.
- **Universal Witness Competency (1792–1793):** By the Judicial Regulation of April 27, 1792 (codified as Section 56 of Bengal Regulation IX 1793), the Company decreed that the religious persuasion of witnesses could no longer disqualify their testimony, invalidating traditional Hanafi rules excluding non-Muslim witnesses.

To enforce these statutory overrides within the regular court system, the Company established the mechanism of the **"hypothetical second** _**futwa**_**."** When a criminal trial was conducted before a Circuit Court, the native Muslim law officer (_maulvi_ or _kazi_) delivered a formal legal ruling (_futwa_) stating whether the accused was guilty under Islamic law. If the _maulvi_ issued an acquittal based on Hanafi procedural restrictions—such as the key witness being non-Muslim, or the victim's heirs refusing to prosecute—the European judge was forbidden from releasing the prisoner.

Instead, under **Bengal Regulations IV & XIV of 1797**, **Regulation VIII of 1799**, **Regulation VIII of 1801**, and **Regulation LIII of 1803**, the law officer was legally required to issue a secondary, hypothetical _futwa_. In this second ruling, the law officer was forced to declare what punishment the prisoner would have received _if_ the legal impediment had not existed (e.g., assuming the non-Muslim witness had been a Muslim, or assuming the victim's heirs had demanded _kisas_). If the hypothetical second _futwa_ indicated guilt, the European judge was authorized to pass sentence or refer the record to the _Nizamut Adawlut_ for exemplary punishment.

This reliance on legal fictions progressively marginalized native law officers within the trial process:

- **Bengal Regulation XVII 1817 (Section 4):** Empowered the Justices of the _Nizamut Adawlut_ to explicitly override a _futwa_ of acquittal issued by a law officer if the judges were satisfied that the prisoner's guilt was established by the evidentiary record.
- **Bengal Regulation IV 1822 (Sections 1 & 2):** Extended this executive power, enabling the _Nizamut Adawlut_ to override a _futwa_ of conviction if the judges deemed the ruling unsupported by the evidence.
- **Bengal Regulation VI 1832:** Dealt a decisive blow to native legal officers by authorizing European judges presiding over criminal trials to substitute native assessors, punchayets, or juries for Muslim law officers, explicitly declaring that judges were no longer required to seek or abide by a _futwa_.
- **Madras Act I 1840 & Act XI 1864:** Madras formally absolved its _Foujdary Adawlut_ from consulting law officers for _futwas_ in 1840, culminating in **Act XI of 1864**, which abolished the offices of Hindu _pundit_ and Muslim _kazi_ across all Company courts, dismantling the era of legal fictions and Islamic procedural workarounds.

With the traditional legal apparatus neutralized, colonial administrators shifted their focus toward constructing statutory definitions for collective, gang-based offenses.

### 2. Conceptual and Statutory Architecture of Gang-Based Criminalization

#### 2.1 Deconstructing Legal Definitions: Dacoity vs. Thagi

As the East India Company consolidated its territorial grip, administrators struggled to classify collective violent offenses within standard legal categories. To maintain social control along trade routes and within rural districts, the colonial legal machinery constructed precise statutory definitions for two distinct categories of gang-based crime: **dacoity** and **thagi**.

##### Etymological Origins and Statutory Gang Thresholds

##### Dacoity

Derived from the Hindi term _dakaity_—tracing back to the verb _dakna_ ("to shout") or the Sanskrit _dashta_ ("pressed together")—dacoity was defined by colonial law as open robbery committed by an armed gang. The earliest comprehensive statutory definition appeared in **Bengal Regulation LIII 1803** (and its duplicate, **Madras Regulation XV 1803**), which designated the offense as "robbery by open violence" (translating the Islamic legal terms _suruca-i-kubra_, _shubkhoone_, or _dukaytee_). The regulation defined the crime as going forth in a gang with offensive weapons, by day or night, with the criminal intent of committing robbery by open force or intimidation.

The statutory threshold for gang size evolved systematically. In Macaulay’s draft Penal Code of 1837, a dacoity gang was defined as requiring a minimum of six persons. In the final **Indian Penal Code of 1860** (Section 391), this threshold was fixed at five or more persons acting conjointly.

##### Thagi

Derived from the Hindi word _thag_—traditionally meaning a cheat, swindler, or trickster—the term underwent a radical redefinition within colonial legal discourse. Rather than simple swindlers, _thugs_ were redefined by Company officials as members of secret, hereditary fraternities of professional murderers who strangled unwary travelers on the high roads using a cloth noose (_phansi_, hence their regional designation as _phansigars_).

In his landmark 1816 study, _Of the Murderers Called Phansigars_, Dr. Robert C. Sherwood documented the regional vernacular terms used across South India to identify these gang stranglers: **Phansigars** or **Thugs** in Hindustani, **Ari Tulucar** (Tamil, meaning "Mussulman noosers"), **Tanti Calleru** (Canarese, meaning "thieves who use a wire or catgut noose"), and **Warlu Wahndlu** / **Warlu Vayshay Wahndloo** (Telugu, meaning "people who use the noose").

As Captain William H. Sleeman and the Thagi Department expanded their operations in the 1830s, official reports identified several specialized sub-categories:

- _**Dhutooreas**_ **or** _**Meetawallas**_**:** Professional poisoners who administered _datura_ seeds or chemical plant extracts in food or drink to incapacitate or kill travelers before plundering them.
- _**Megpunnas**_**:** Gangs specializing in murdering parents to abduct and sell their young children into domestic slavery or prostitution.
- **Secret Religious/Hereditary Fraternities:** Groups described in official literature as performing rituals dedicated to the goddess Bhowani prior to launching murderous expeditions.

##### Pre-Colonial Genealogies and Maratha State Integration

Colonial literature depicted thagi as an ancient religious cult operating outside civil society. However, historical documentation reveals complex pre-colonial genealogies and state integration. In his 1834 report to Agent F.C. Smith, D.F. Macleod traced thug lineages back 15 generations to Muslim families who settled among the Lodhee tribe and the "Chouras" (84 vagrant tribes) near Delhi.

Furthermore, research by Kim Wagner identified a crucial 1797 Maratha administrative document listing 318 families from the Sindouse pargana (Etawah district) who paid a formal **"Thug Tax"** (_Thuggee Nuzzerana_) to the Maratha state. Far from being invisible cultists, these groups were recognized, integrated, and taxed by pre-colonial polities as specialized seasonal plunderers before the Company re-categorized them as absolute outlaws.

##### Statutory Expansion and Administrative Friction

A central administrative struggle facing William Sleeman was bringing all forms of chemical poisoning (_dhutooreas_) and child-stealing (_megpunnas_) under the statutory framework governing thagi. Sleeman lobbied for a declaratory act explicitly defining poisoners as thugs. However, his efforts encountered institutional resistance from executive and judicial authorities who were reluctant to extend extraordinary dragnet procedures beyond road strangulation.

To resolve these legal ambiguities, the Government enacted **Act III of 1848**. This statute defined a thug as any person habitually associated with others for the purpose of committing, by means likely to cause death, "the offence of child-stealing or the offence of Robbery not amounting to Dacoity." Despite this enactment, the Government of India repeatedly refused requests from Sleeman's successor, Lt. Col. Charles Hervey (in 1865, 1868, and 1873), to deploy the Thagi Department's approver system against ordinary _dhutoorea_ poisoners.

#### 2.2 Socio-Economic Realities vs. Colonial Constructs

Modern historiography has exposed a vast gulf between the official colonial narrative regarding dacoits and thugs and the socio-economic realities of 19th-century rural India.

##### Socio-Economic Drivers of Rural Banditry

Colonial policy framed gang robbery as the innate expression of hereditary criminal communities. In reality, dacoity was deeply tied to the socio-economic dislocations generated by the Company’s land revenue systems and administrative restructuring:

- **Displacement of Village Watchmen (**_**Chaukidars**_**):** Following the Permanent Settlement of 1793 in Bengal, the Company restructured rural property rights. New revenue farmers (_zamindars_) and salaried police officers (_darogahs_) replaced traditional landholders. In this process, thousands of village watchmen (_chaukidars_) and village guards (_paiks_) were stripped of their ancestral service lands (_paikan_). Deprived of their livelihoods, many former _chaukidars_ joined or led (_sirdars_) rural dacoit gangs, deploying their intimate knowledge of local geography and village administration to survive.
- **Agrarian Seasonality and Famine:** In the Madras Presidency, empirical studies demonstrate a direct correlation between grain prices, climate cycles, and dacoity rates. Dacoity rates spiked during the hot, dry months preceding the monsoon, when agricultural labor ceased, grain reserves fell, and food prices peaked. Cattle raids and grain-looting were survival strategies deployed by impoverished agrarian laborers.
- **Political Disruption:** The collapse of the Mughal Empire and Maratha polities during the late 18th century left large numbers of demobilized soldiers, mercenaries, and irregular camp followers without employment, driving them into highway robbery across Central India and Malwa.

##### Deconstructing the Thug Profile

The official narrative depicted thugs as members of a fanatical, high-caste religious cult committed to ritual strangulation. However, empirical analysis of trial depositions recorded between 1810 and 1850 reveals a different social reality. Christopher Kenna's tabular analysis of Sagar and Jabalpur trial records (1831–1836) demonstrates that out of 995 convicted prisoners, 31 percent were Muslims, 30 percent were Lodhis, and 7 percent were Brahmins, with the remainder spread across Ahirs, Chamars, and low-caste laborers.

Rather than being full-time cultists, accused thugs were ordinary members of agrarian society—agricultural laborers, peddlers, low-caste artisans (such as _manihars_ or Muslim glass-bangle makers), demobilized soldiers, and mendicants—who engaged in seasonal robbery to supplement meager agrarian incomes.

##### Primary Source Voice: The Deposition of Poorun Phansigar

The deposition of captured thug **Poorun Phansigar** (1829) vividly illustrates how pre-colonial travel dynamics operated prior to British pacification:

"It was before the establishment of tranquillity over the Country that...our excursions were neither carried to so great a distance as they have since been, nor were so lucrative or certain, for in those days travellers, particularly with much property seldom ventured to go from one place to another without being well escorted or in large parties and we feared the Pindaries and other mounted plunderers as much as other classes did."

This primary deposition demonstrates that Company pacification altered road security and travel patterns, making unwary, small groups of travelers more vulnerable to seasonal stranglers.

##### The Administrative Shift toward "Criminal Tribes"

As the Company consolidated its authority, its policy toward roving, armed populations underwent a major shift. Armed hill bands and itinerant communities—such as the Ramoosies and Kolis of the Western Ghats, and the Budducks, Kechuks, and Bowreeas of Northern India—were initially treated as local insurgents or displaced social units. By the 1840s, executive authorities stopped treating these groups as political actors and began classifying them as habitual, biological criminals.

By categorizing entire communities as hereditary "robbers by profession," the Company constructed an administrative framework for social surveillance. This legal logic created a direct lineage leading to the post-1857 Crown administration's **Criminal Tribes Act (Act XXVII of 1871)**, which subjected designated communities to mandatory registration, restricted movement, and involuntary resettlement.

#### 2.3 The Evolution of Dragnet Legislation: From Specific Acts to Gang Association

To suppress dacoity and thagi, the East India Company enacted dragnet legislation that altered Anglo-Indian criminal jurisprudence. This legislative evolution shifted the focus of criminal law from proving an individual's commission of a specific substantive crime to penalizing simple membership in or association with a designated gang.

- **Security for Good Behaviour (1818–1819):** Under **Bengal Regulation VIII of 1818** and **Bengal Regulation III of 1819**, magistrates were empowered to demand financial security for future good behaviour from individuals classified as "notorious" dacoits, thugs, or highway robbers. If the accused could not provide security—which was virtually impossible for impoverished suspects—the magistrate was authorized to detain them in prison indefinitely without proving a specific criminal act.
- **The Landmark Gang Association Law (Act XXX 1836):** Enacted by the Legislative Council of India under Governor-General Lord Auckland, **Act XXX of 1836** represented a expansion of colonial state power. Section I established that anyone proved to have belonged to "any gang of Thugs" was guilty of an offense punishable by life imprisonment with hard labor. The law eliminated the requirement to prove that the accused had committed a specific act of murder or robbery. Membership in a gang was itself sufficient for conviction. Section III abolished _futwas_ from Muslim law officers in thagi trials.
- **Extension to Dacoits (Act XXIV 1843):** Directing the dragnet against rural dacoity, **Act XXIV of 1843** extended the principles of Act XXX of 1836 to dacoit gangs. Section I declared that anyone proved to have belonged to "any gang of Dacoits" was liable to transportation for life or rigorous imprisonment, eliminating the requirement to prove a specific robbery.
- **Extension to Vagrant Gangs (Act XI 1848):** The dragnet reached its maximum scope with **Act XI of 1848**, which applied the trial procedures and penalties of the anti-thagi and dacoity acts to "any wandering gang of persons associated for the purposes of theft or robbery, not being a gang of Thugs or Dacoits."

| Statute Title        | Target Group                               | Standard of Proof Required                                                                                         | Statutory Penalty                                                                                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Act XXX of 1836**  | Anyone belonging to a "gang of Thugs."     | Proof of simple association with/membership in a gang; **no proof of specific act of murder or robbery required.** | Imprisonment for life with hard labor; universal presidency jurisdiction; elimination of _futwas_.  |
| **Act XXIV of 1843** | Anyone belonging to a "gang of Dacoits."   | Proof of simple belonging to a dacoit gang; **elimination of specific substantive offense requirement.**           | Transportation for life, or imprisonment with hard labor up to 10 years; elimination of _futwas_.   |
| **Act XI of 1848**   | "Wandering gangs" of thieves and vagrants. | Proof of habitual association in a wandering gang for the purpose of theft or robbery.                             | Rigorous imprisonment; subject to summary commitment and trial procedures of previous dragnet acts. |

Executing these dragnet laws required specialized administrative agencies and novel evidentiary frameworks.

### 3. Procedural Innovations and the Imperial Dragnet

#### 3.1 The Approver System and Partial Pardons

The operational engine of the Thagi and Dacoity Department was the **approver system**. Because secret gang crimes yielded few independent witnesses willing to risk retribution by testifying in court, colonial authorities relied on turncoat suspects—known as approvers, _goyendas_, or _gorindas_—to secure convictions.

##### Legal Mechanics of the Partial Pardon Framework

Devised by F.C. Smith (Agent to the Governor-General in the Saugor and Nerbudda Territories), Captain William H. Sleeman, and Henry Shakespeare, the approver system rested on an executive compromise. Under regular Company regulations (**Bengal Regulation X of 1824** and **Madras Regulation VIII of 1802**), an accomplice who accepted a state pardon and provided testimony was entitled to full release. However, colonial authorities recognized that releasing self-confessed thugs or dacoits was politically unacceptable.

To resolve this issue, Smith, Sleeman, and Shakespeare devised the **partial or conditional pardon framework**. Under this system, captured gang members were offered an executive deal: if they delivered a full confession naming all accomplices, detailed their gang's history, and assisted in capturing fugitives, the state would commute their sentence from execution or transportation to lifelong detention.

##### Institutional Fate of Approvers: The Jubbulpore School of Industry

Approvers and their families were permanently detained at specialized facilities, most notably the **Jubbulpore School of Industry** (established in 1838). There, approvers and their children were trained in industrial trades, manufacturing tents, weaves, and heavy carpets under lifelong surveillance.

Demonstrating the Benthamite ideal of reforming "criminal families" into productive industrial laborers, one of the massive carpets hand-woven by Jubbulpore approvers was famously installed in the Waterloo Room at Windsor Castle.

##### Macaulay’s Benthamite Validation (Act XIX 1837)

The approver system encountered legal challenges regarding witness competency. Under traditional rules of evidence, a convicted felon was legally incompetent to testify in court. To resolve this without creating a degraded code of evidence specifically for thagi, Thomas Babington Macaulay drafted **Act XIX of 1837**.

Applying Benthamite principles of legal universality, Act XIX declared that no person, by reason of any conviction for any offense whatever, should be incompetent to be a witness in any court within the territories of the East India Company. This allowed approver testimony to be legally admitted in all criminal trials across British India.

##### Ethical Risks and Systemic Abuses

While effective at securing convictions, the approver system generated severe systemic abuses:

- **Coerced Confessions and Coaching:** Native police subordinates (_darogahs_) routinely coached approvers, feeding them names and dates to ensure consistent testimony in court.
- **Perjury and False Denunciation:** Approvers quickly realized that their physical survival and comfort depended on their utility to the Department. This incentive structure led to perjury, false denunciations of personal enemies, and the implication of innocent relatives.
- **Indefinite Pre-Trial Detention:** Suspects denounced by approvers were held in prison for years without trial while departmental officers sought corroborative evidence.

#### 3.2 Extra-Territorial Jurisdiction and Suppression Department Mechanics

To execute its dragnet campaigns, the East India Company created a centralized, extra-territorial apparatus that operated across traditional legal boundaries.

##### Specialized Agency Structure

Established by Governor-General Lord William Bentinck in 1829, the **Thagi Department** (expanded in 1839 to become the **Thagi and Dacoity Department**) functioned as a quasi-military intelligence agency. Operating directly under the Political Department rather than local district courts, the Department was headed by a General Superintendent (William Sleeman) supervising regional Assistant Superintendents across British presidencies and Princely States.

##### Legislative Removal of Jurisdictional Boundaries

- **Universal Jurisdiction (Act XXX 1836 & Act XVIII 1837):** **Act XXX of 1836** and **Act XVIII of 1837** stripped away traditional venue and jurisdictional limits in thagi trials. Section II of Act XXX empowered any court in British India to try any accused thug regardless of where the crime was committed. Act XVIII of 1837 explicitly authorized specialized magisterial officers of the Thagi Department to arrest, commit, and try suspects in any presidency, superseding district court boundaries.
- **Extra-Territorial Detention (Act XVIII 1843):** Prior to 1843, Company jails could not legally detain individuals convicted by courts operating in Princely States. **Act XVIII of 1843** resolved this limitation by authorizing Company jails to receive and hold prisoners convicted of thagi or dacoity in Princely States (such as Gwalior) under tribunals presided over by British political officers, overriding native sovereignty.

##### Operational Machinery and Archives

The operations of the Thagi and Dacoity Department relied on specialized military units and centralized records:

- _**Tomaun of Nujeebs**_**:** A dedicated force of semi-disciplined native infantry militia (_nujeebs_) assigned to Captain Sleeman to conduct armed raids, guard prisoners, and pursue fleeing gangs across Princely State frontiers.
- **Centralized Intelligence Archives:** Sleeman established a card-index and mapping system at Jubbulpore. This included genealogical registers of suspected criminal families, cross-referenced registers of gang confessions, and his publication of the _**Ramaseeana**_ (1836)—a dictionary of the secret argot (_Ramasee_) utilized by thugs across India. These intelligence files allowed departmental officers to verify approver confessions and track suspects across regional boundaries.

#### 3.3 Statutory Chronology of Thagi and Dacoity Enactments (1803–1848)

- **Bengal Regulation LIII 1803 / Madras Regulation XV 1803:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Defined "robbery by open violence" (_suruca-i-kubra_, _dukaytee_); overrode Hanafi exceptions barring _hadd_; introduced the "hypothetical second _futwa_"; established tiered sentences (death for robbery with murder; life transportation for robbery with maiming; 14 years imprisonment for basic gang robbery).
  - _Operational Impact:_ Established statutory legal fictions and shifted gang violence prosecutions into the realm of public justice.
- **Bengal Regulation IX 1808 / Madras Regulation XIII 1809:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Authorized magistrates to publicly proclaim dacoit leaders (_sirdars_); granted immunity to anyone wounding or killing a proclaimed dacoit who resisted or fled; mandated automatic life transportation for proclaimed dacoits who failed to surrender within two months.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Created an outlawry regime that suspended regular trial protections for suspected dacoit leaders.
- **Bengal Regulation VIII 1818 / Bengal Regulation III 1819:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Empowered magistrates to demand security for good behaviour from "notorious" dacoits, thugs, and highway robbers; authorized indefinite detention without proof of a specific crime if security was not furnished.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Laid the statutory foundation for status-based pre-trial detention.
- **Act XXX of 1836:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Criminalized simple membership in "any gang of Thugs" with life imprisonment with hard labor; eliminated the requirement to prove a specific act of murder or robbery; abolished _futwas_ for thagi trials; established universal presidency jurisdiction.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Shifted Anglo-Indian jurisprudence from individual criminal acts to status-based gang criminalization.
- **Acts XVIII and XIX of 1837:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Act XVIII authorized specialized Thagi Department officers to commit suspects for trial across all presidencies; Act XIX made convicted felons competent witnesses in all courts.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Legitimized the approver system and empowered the Thagi Department to operate across presidency boundaries.
- **Act XV of 1838:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Repealed Clause 1, Section XXXV of Bombay Regulation XII of 1827, which had restricted conditional pardons.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Removed Bombay Presidency legal protections, allowing the Thagi Department to deploy its partial-pardon approver framework in Bombay courts.
- **Act XVIII of 1839:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Applied the dragnet trial procedures of the 1836 and 1837 Thagi Acts to individuals accused of receiving or buying property stolen by thugs.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Targeted the economic support networks and fencing operations supporting highway gangs.
- **Act XVIII of 1843:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Authorized East India Company jails to hold convicts sentenced for thagi or dacoity by British political officers operating in Princely States.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Expanded British extra-territorial authority over sovereign Indian polities.
- **Act XXIV of 1843:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Extended the gang membership penalties of Act XXX of 1836 to dacoit gangs; made simple belonging to a "gang of Dacoits" punishable by life transportation or rigorous imprisonment; eliminated _futwas_ in dacoity trials.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Unified the statutory dragnet frameworks governing dacoity and thagi.
- **Act IV of 1844:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Repealed Bengal Regulation IX of 1808 (the Proclamation Regulation).
  - _Operational Impact:_ Ended the 1808 outlawry framework, which high-ranking police officials admitted had become obsolete and excessively severe.
- **Act II of 1847:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Abolished the practice of _godna_ (tattooing or branding the foreheads or bodies of perjury convicts and repeat felons) across Bengal, Bombay, and Madras.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Marked a shift from physical corporal marking to administrative surveillance via registries, photography, and centralized intelligence files.
- **Act III of 1848:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Clarified statutory definitions to explicitly include _dhutoorea_ poisoners and _megpunna_ child-stealers under the legal definition of a "Thug."
  - _Operational Impact:_ Expanded the Thagi Department's statutory reach over non-strangling gang offenses.
- **Act XI of 1848:**
  - _Key Provisions:_ Penalized simple membership in non-thug/dacoit "wandering gangs" of thieves and vagrants with rigorous imprisonment.
  - _Operational Impact:_ Broadened status-based dragnet penalization to include itinerant populations and nomadic communities.

This rapid expansion of executive dragnet laws generated intense friction with regular judicial authorities.

### 4. Institutional Conflicts, Legal Limits, and Jurisprudential Legacy

#### 4.1 Executive Dragnet vs. Judicial Oversight: The Bombay Sudder Court and District Resistance

The expansion of the Thagi and Dacoity Department generated friction between military executive officers and the regular judiciary. District magistrates and High Court judges defended due process, local codes, and evidentiary standards against executive dragnet practices.

##### Opposition from the Bombay Sudder Court

The most persistent opposition to the Thagi Department's extra-territorial powers came from the judges of the Bombay _Sudder Foujdary Adawlut_ (led by Puisne Judge P.W. LeGeyt). When the Supreme Government enacted Acts XXX of 1836 and XVIII of 1837, the Bombay judges raised strong objections:

- **Invasion of Rights under Elphinstone’s Code:** The judges argued that sending Bombay citizens for trial before extra-territorial tribunals at Jubbulpore or Agra under Act XXX of 1836 violated Chapter I of **Bombay Regulation XI of 1827**, which protected Bombay subjects from foreign jurisdiction. They warned that citizens risked being removed from their home districts and tried under unfamiliar legal standards.
- **Distrust of Uncorroborated Approvers:** The judges emphasized that under Clause 2, Section XXXV of **Bombay Regulation XII of 1827**, the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice was legally insufficient for conviction. They argued that relying solely on approver evidence would lead to systemic perjury and false accusations.
- **Criticism of Military Officers as Judges:** The Bombay court criticized the appointment of military officers (such as Sleeman and his assistants) as extra-territorial judges, arguing that officers lacking judicial training were unequipped to evaluate evidence fairly.

##### District Resistance: Allan Octavian Hume's Etawah Critique

Following the 1857 Uprising, Allan Octavian Hume, Magistrate of Etawah, launched a critique of the Thagi and Dacoity Department's operations in his district:

- **Systemic Abuses:** Hume exposed widespread abuses, demonstrating that departmental approvers were routinely coached by corrupt native subordinates to falsely implicate innocent individuals.
- **Indefinite Detention and the Gunga Sing Case:** Hume highlighted cases such as that of **Gunga Sing**, an approver held in custody without trial for over a year and threatened with capital punishment for refusing to implicate his close relatives. Hume argued that such arbitrary practices drove local populations to support anti-British insurgents during 1857.

This conflict highlighted a fundamental divide within the colonial state: a clash between executive military expediency—which prioritized summary containment—and judicial oversight, which sought to maintain legal standards and due process.

#### 4.2 Rationalizing Legislative Limits: Sorcery, Child Ornaments, and Educational Alternatives

While the East India Company deployed dragnet legislation against dacoity and thagi, the Law Commission explicitly declined to pass special penal statutes for other social practices. This distinction demonstrated that colonial intervention was bounded by practical governance concerns and public order considerations.

##### Sorcery and Witch-Hunting

Incidents of witch-killing were common in frontier tracts, such as Ramgurh (among the Sutar caste) and Malabar (where lower-caste _pariar_ slaves were targeted as sorcerers).

##### Restoring Vivid Primary Details: The 1792 Ramgurh Trial

In the 1792 Ramgurh trial under Governor Sir John Shore (codified in **Bengal Regulation IV 1797, Section VI**), three men of the Sutar caste were tried for murdering five women accused of witchcraft. The villagers refused to prosecute, citing **three distinct trials by ordeal** used to identify the witches:

1. Marked Saul tree branches planted in water for 4.5 hours (branches that withered identified the witch).
2. Packets of rice marked with women's names placed in white ant nests (rice consumed by ants identified the witch).
3. Mustard seed oil dropped into water leaf-cups under lamplight (shadows appearing on the water condemned the woman to death).

##### Restoring Vivid Primary Details: Malabar Court Records

In 1834–1836, Circuit Judge A.D. Campbell documented witch-hunting atrocities among lower-caste _Pariar_ slaves in Malabar:

- An 1829 trial involving two slaves accused of conjuring an earthen pot containing a piece of calf skin into a woman's womb.
- The **Nilgiri Hills massacre**, where a mob bound 15 _Pariar_ slaves with hands behind their backs, immersed them in a river near suffocation, forced their children to rub sand into their parents' wounds, demolished their village, and banished the survivors.

##### Law Commission Rejection

Despite these atrocities, when Madras judges urged the Law Commission in 1837 to enact a statute criminalizing the _practice_ of sorcery itself, Macaulay and Cameron rejected the proposal. Citing Benthamite principles, the Law Commission reasoned that passing a law against practicing sorcery would signal that the state recognized magic as real. The Commission concluded that the state should only punish the substantive crime of murder resulting from witch-hunts (under Bengal Regulation IV 1797 and the Elphinstone Code), relying on public instruction rather than penal law to address superstitious beliefs.

##### Murder of Children for Ornaments

In 1835, the Madras _Foujdary Adawlut_ proposed a law authorizing police to confiscate gold and silver ornaments from children found wandering unaccompanied in public, aiming to prevent murders committed to steal jewellery.

In 1838, the Law Commission rejected the proposal. The Commission offered a Benthamite defense of parental vanity, arguing that decorating children with jewellery was an economic driver that stimulated trade, industry, and metalwork.

Furthermore, an empirical survey revealed that child ornament murders were far less prevalent than claimed (totaling 255 cases across India over three years). Officials warned that the law would provoke public hostility and enable police extortion. The Government instead reissued a **1818 Nizamut Adawlut Circular Order**, relying on public advisories and press notices to warn parents to protect their children.

##### Hook-Swinging (_Charak Puja_)

The practice of hook-swinging—where devotees were suspended from poles by iron hooks inserted into their back muscles—was viewed by European officials as a public disturbance. However, during the Company era, the Law Commission repeatedly declined to enact explicit penal statutes banning the ritual. Instead, executive officers relied on informal advisories, local magistrate orders, and landlord pressure to suppress the practice without passing formal penal laws.

#### 4.3 Codification, the Indian Penal Code (1860), and the Long Shadow of Criminalization

The piecemeal regulations and special acts forged during the anti-thagi and dacoity campaigns served as direct testing grounds for formal codification under the British Crown. Following the transfer of authority from the East India Company to the Crown in 1858, the legal experiments of the early 19th century were permanently integrated into the **Indian Penal Code (Act XLV of 1860)**, drafted by Macaulay and revised by Barnes Peacock.

##### Permanent Codification of Dragnet Principles in the IPC

The emergency powers devised by the Thagi and Dacoity Department were permanently integrated into the substantive criminal law of British India:

- **Sections 310 & 311 (Thuggee):** Section 310 incorporated the legal definition established by Act III of 1848, defining a thug as anyone habitually associated with others for the purpose of committing robbery or child-stealing accompanied by murder. Section 311 made the status of being a thug punishable by life imprisonment and fine.
- **Sections 391 & 395–400 (Dacoity):** Section 391 defined dacoity as conjoint robbery committed by five or more persons. Sections 395 through 399 established severe penalties for dacoity, while **Section 400** permanently codified the dragnet principle of Act XXIV of 1843: simple membership in a gang of dacoits was made punishable by life imprisonment or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years, without requiring proof of a specific robbery.

##### Institutional Legacy and the "Long Shadow"

The institutional mechanics created during the anti-thagi era left a lasting mark on state surveillance in South Asia:

- **Central Criminal Intelligence Department:** When the Thagi and Dacoity Department ceased operations within British India in 1860, its extra-territorial intelligence units continued operating within Princely States under the Foreign Department until 1904. Its archives, genealogical mapping practices, and surveillance systems were directly transferred into the newly created **Central Criminal Intelligence Department**, laying the foundation for modern intelligence gathering in South Asia.
- **The Criminal Tribes Act (Act XXVII of 1871):** The logic of status-based criminalization reached its conclusion in the **Criminal Tribes Act of 1871**. This law enabled provincial governments to register entire castes, tribes, or itinerant communities as "criminal tribes," subjecting their members to mandatory reporting, restricted movement, and forced adult rehabilitation settlements—a direct legacy of the administrative approaches developed during the Budduck and Kechuk campaigns.

##### Jurisprudential Assessment: Rule of Law vs. Ad-Hoc Executive Power

Ultimately, the East India Company’s criminal legislation reveals a tension between procedural legal reform and colonial power. Through the expansion of executive authority, the Company constructed a system designed to secure social control under the banner of public justice.

While its legal architects invoked Benthamite utilitarian ideas of clarity, universality, and certainty, the resulting statutory framework compromised foundational principles of individual accountability. By shifting the legal standard from individual guilt to status-based association, Company legal policy established a framework of executive dragnet powers whose impact shaped South Asian criminal jurisprudence well into the post-colonial era.

## **Historical Analysis: The Transformation of Islamic Criminal Jurisprudence into Anglo-Indian Law under the East India Company** {#historical-analysis}

### 1. Foundations of Legal Administration and Early Judicial Dualism (1765–1790)

The acquisition of the _Diwani_ of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa by the East India Company (EIC) in 1765 following the Treaty of Allahabad marked a fundamental realignment in British commercial and political engagement with South Asia. Confronted with the immense administrative burden of extracting land revenue and maintaining public order over millions of Indian subjects, Company officials recognized that an abrupt imposition of foreign English legal codes would destabilize the indigenous social framework and disrupt the revenue collection apparatus. Operating as a nominal vassal of the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, the Company adopted a pragmatically conservative strategy: maintaining existing indigenous legal frameworks while placing European supervisors at the apex of judicial oversight. Civil administration was progressively absorbed into Company administrative structures, but criminal justice (_Nizamut_) was consciously preserved under the purview of modified Islamic law (_Sharia_) administered by indigenous law officers. This deliberate system of judicial dualism aimed to minimize local disaffection, preserve administrative continuity, and legitimize Company authority through established Mughal institutional channels.

#### 1.1 The Genesis of Company Jurisdiction and the Reserved List

The statutory authorization for British judicial authority in India evolved across nearly two centuries of royal charters and parliamentary enactments. Early royal charters granted by King Charles II in 1661 and 1668 empowered the Company to exercise rudimentary civil and criminal jurisdiction within its isolated factory settlements. A structural shift occurred with the Letters Patent of 1726, which established royal Mayor’s Courts and Courts of Quarter Sessions in the three presidency towns of Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay. These tribunals were formally instituted by royal authority rather than Company delegation, introducing English statute and common law procedure—including petty and grand juries (restricted to Christian residents for another century) and civil probate jurisdiction—into the urban presidency enclaves. The Governor and Council were endowed with limited legislative power, provided their local regulations did not contradict the laws of England.

Outside the presidency towns, in the rural hinterland (_mofussil_), the Company's assumption of the _Diwani_ necessitated a distinct system for administering justice to the indigenous population. In 1772, Governor-General Warren Hastings instituted his "Plan for the Administration of Justice," seeking to systematically reorganize mofussil litigation. A crucial pillar of Hastings' strategy was the famous 23rd Rule, formally enacted into the Bengal Government's Judicial Regulation of 11 April 1780 (Section 27). The 23rd Rule established a statutory "reserved list" of personal law subjects:

> "That in all suits regarding inheritance, marriage and caste and other religious usages or institutions, the laws of the Koran with respect to Mahomedans, and those of the Shaster with respect to Gentoos shall be invariably adhered to."

In 1781, Chief Justice Elijah Impey revised the procedural rules for the Company's _Sudder Dewannee Adawlut_ and _Mofussil Dewannee Adawlut_ courts via Judicial Regulation VI of 1781. Impey amended Hastings' reserved list by explicitly adding the word "succession" to the reserved subjects (Section 37). Furthermore, Section 60 of Impey's regulation introduced the famous Residual Formula, directing mofussil judges to act according to "justice, equity and good conscience" in all cases where no specific statutory rule or personal law applied.

Simultaneously, the British Parliament enacted the Declaratory Act of 1781 (21 Geo III c. 70) to clarify the contentious jurisdictional boundaries between the royal Supreme Court of Judicature at Calcutta and the Company's mofussil courts. Section 17 of 21 Geo III c. 70 entrenched Hastings' principle in statute law for Indian residents of Calcutta, but shifted the text's emphasis toward commercial stability by listing "inheritance and succession to lands, rents and goods and all matters of contract and dealing between party and party," while omitting explicit mention of marriage, caste, and religious usages. Section 17 also introduced the "law of the defendant" for inter-religious disputes, requiring that when parties belonged to different faiths, the case must be determined by the legal system of the defendant. Section 18 further safeguarded indigenous domestic authority by decreeing that acts performed under caste and family rules should not be judged as crimes under English law.

The Cornwallis Code of 1793 formally re-consolidated these principles in mofussil statutory law under Section 15 of Bengal Regulation IV of 1793. This regulation restated the full list of reserved personal subjects from Impey’s 1781 regulation but modernized the nomenclature, referring broadly to "Mahomedan laws" and "Hindoo Laws" rather than the original textual references to the "Koran" and the "Shaster."

#### 1.2 The "Venal Law Officers" Paradigm vs. Judicial Realities

To operationalize this policy of dualism, European judges—who lacked formal legal training, proficiency in classical languages, and familiarity with local customs—were required to consult native law officers attached to the courts. Hindu _pandits_ delivered written legal opinions (_vyavasthas_) on the _Shastras_, while Muslim _kazis_ and _maulvis_ declaimed the _Sharia_ through formal legal rulings (_fatwas_).

Under Hastings' sponsorship, eleven _pandits_ worked in Calcutta between May 1773 and February 1775 to compile an authoritative digest of Hindu law titled _Vivadarnave-setu_ ("Bridge Across the Ocean of Litigation"), which was translated into Persian and subsequently into English by Nathaniel Brassey Halhed as _A Code of Gentoo Laws_ (1776). Later, Sir William Jones sponsored a more extensive digest compiled by Jagannatha Tarkapancanana, titled _Vivada-bhangarnava_ ("Ocean of Resolutions of Disputes"), which was translated into English by Henry Thomas Colebrooke as the _Digest of Hindu Law on Contracts and Successions_ (1798).

As English court procedure increasingly relied on recorded precedents, European judges expressed growing frustration over inconsistent rulings from native law officers. _Pandits_ routinely produced divergent _vyavasthas_ and _kazis_ delivered conflicting _fatwas_ across different courts or within the same tribunal over time. British judicial figures constructed a narrative of pervasive native corruption and mendacity. Chief Justice Elijah Impey remarked during the Patna Case that native law officers were "mean, weak, ignorant and corrupt," noting that the Principal _Kazi_ received a monthly salary of no more than 100 rupees. Sir William Jones, in an influential 19 March 1788 letter to Lord Cornwallis, asserted that he "could not with an easy conscience concur in a decision merely on the written opinion of native lawyers in any cause in which they could have the remotest interest in misleading the Court." This suspicion was perpetuated in the early nineteenth century by Sir Francis W. Macnaghten in his _Considerations on the Hindoo Law_ (1824) and his son, William Hay Macnaghten, whose _Sudder Dewanny Adawlut Reports_ featured accounts of _pandits_ delivering fraudulent legal opinions influenced by bribes.

Modern historiographical reassessments, notably by scholars such as J.D.M. Derrett and S. Venkataraman, demonstrate that this Eurocentric paradigm of "venal law officers" misconstrued the core mechanics of indigenous jurisprudence. British administrators approached Indian legal traditions through an English statutory and common law framework, operating under the assumption that Hindu and Muslim laws were fixed, text-based codes analogous to Western statutory systems. Sir William Jones romanticized Hindu law as immemorially ancient and fixed, assuming that Brahmins acted as authoritative priests administering a single statutory code. In his subsequent translations and commentaries, Henry Thomas Colebrooke erroneously reinforced the idea that Hindu law was divided into rigid regional "schools" (_Mitakshara_ and _Dayabhaga_) analogous to Sunni _Fiqh_ schools. This textual reductionism bound a dynamic, living science of _Dharmasastra_ to static treatises, overlooking the reality that Hindu law was deeply rooted in flexible, evolving regional customs (_desachara_) and family traditions, where _smrti_ texts served as analytical guides rather than rigid codes.

Similarly, British administrators overlooked the multifaceted sources of Sunni Islamic jurisprudence (_Fiqh_). Beyond the primary text of the Quran, Hanafi law incorporated three additional authoritative sources:

1. _Hadith_ (recorded prophetic traditions and sayings),
2. _Ijma_ (consensus of legal scholars), and
3. _Kiyas_ (analogical reasoning).

Furthermore, Sunni jurisprudence was divided into four distinct schools of thought, while Shia jurisprudence was largely ignored in Bengal until 1810. When native law officers attempted to apply subtle textual interpretation, analogical reasoning, or local customary modifications, European judges interpreted the resulting variations not as legitimate legal reasoning, but as evidence of deceit and venality. As Justice J.B. Norton later acknowledged after the formal abolition of court law officers in 1864, British administrators had "kicked down one of the ladders by which we have climbed," overlooking how native law officers had provided essential flexibility to an otherwise rigid judicial apparatus. Former law officer rulings had allowed Hindu law to function as a living, adaptable legal system, a dynamic process halted when British courts bound indigenous jurisprudence to static textual translations and legal precedents.

This initial reliance on native law officers in civil matters highlighted the operational difficulties of working within indigenous legal systems, leading British administrators to conclude that the criminal justice system (_Nizamut_) required direct statutory intervention under Lord Cornwallis.

### 2. Structural Mechanics of Islamic Criminal Law (_Sharia_) Prior to Company Intervention

When the East India Company assumed criminal jurisdiction over Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa in 1790, it inherited the Islamic penal system based on the Hanafi school of Sunni jurisprudence. Hanafi criminal law possessed a refined conceptual architecture, structured around a fundamental distinction between private rights (_hukuk al-ibad_)—where prosecution and punishment were controlled by the victim or the victim's immediate heirs—and the public sovereign interest (_hukuk Allah_ or state police power). The primary objective of Islamic criminal justice was not the systematic imposition of state coercion, but the restitution of private injury, the preservation of social equilibrium, and the strictly regulated execution of divine ordinances.

#### 2.1 The Four Pillars of Islamic Penal Jurisprudence

Hanafi criminal jurisprudence classified penal sanctions into four primary categories:

1. _**Hadd**_ **(Fixed Mandatory Punishments):** Prescribed penalties for specific crimes deemed transgressions against divine rights. These included stoning or scourging for _zina_ (illicit sexual intercourse), scourging for wine drinking and false accusation of adultery (_kadhf_), amputation of one hand for simple theft (_saraka_), amputation of cross limbs for highway robbery (_kat-al-tariq_), and execution for highway robbery accompanied by murder.
2. _**Kisas**_ **(Retaliation in Kind):** The legal doctrine of exact retaliation, applying primarily to willful homicide and severe physical maiming. _Kisas_ treated homicide as a private injury against the victim's family rather than a public crime against the state.
3. _**Diya**_ **(Blood Money):** Financial compensation substituted for _kisas_. In cases of non-intentional homicide, or where the victim's heirs elected to forgo physical retaliation, the perpetrator paid a fixed monetary fine (_diya_) to the victim's family.
4. _**Tazir**_ / _**Acoobut**_ / _**Seeasut**_ **(Discretionary Punishments):** Discretionary penalties imposed by judges (_kazis_) or magistrates for offenses not explicitly covered by _hadd_ or _kisas_, or where procedural technicalities barred mandatory sentences. _Tazir_ ranged from public reprimand (_tusheer_) to corporal punishment, imprisonment, and banishment.

To balance these severe penalties, Hanafi jurisprudence enforced strict evidentiary procedural rules for _hadd_ and _kisas_. Circumstantial evidence and written documentation were completely inadmissible; convictions depended strictly on voluntary, uncoerced oral confessions or the direct testimony of qualified eyewitnesses. To be legally valid, a confession had to be repeated four separate times before a _kazi_ and could be retracted by the accused at any moment prior to execution. Eyewitness testimony was subject to rigorous qualifications: female witnesses, non-Muslims, slaves, and interested parties were completely barred from testifying in capital or _hadd_ prosecutions.

Because of these restrictive procedural requirements and substantive legal exceptions, convictions for _hadd_ and _kisas_ were frequently barred in practice. Common legal impediments included:

- **Familial Relationship:** _Kisas_ was strictly barred if the perpetrator was a direct ancestor (parent or grandparent) of the victim, or if a master killed his own slave. In such cases, the victim's status was subordinate, rendering physical retaliation legally impossible.
- **Joint Ownership of Stolen Property:** A charge of _hadd_ for theft was barred if the accused possessed any joint ownership, shared claim, or familial relationship to the owner of the stolen goods, as this created legal doubt (_shubha_) regarding absolute ownership.
- **Minimum Value Threshold (**_**Nisab**_**):** _Hadd_ amputation for theft could not be inflicted unless the stolen property was taken from secure custody (_hisn_) and met the minimum value threshold of ten _dirms_ (calculated as equivalent to slightly less than three Bengal sicca rupees).
- **Pardon or Non-Prosecution by Heirs:** Because prosecution for _kisas_ rested entirely with the victim's next of kin, if the heirs chose not to prosecute, pardoned the offender, or accepted _diya_, the criminal courts were legally powerless to inflict capital punishment.
- **Disproportionate Value of Retaliation:** _Kisas_ could not be enforced if the value of the life or limb demanded in retaliation was deemed legally superior to that of the offender, or if one of several co-heirs was a minor or lunatic incapable of demanding execution.

#### 2.2 Private Retribution vs. Sovereign Discretion (_Seeasut_)

The core conceptual friction encountered by early Company administrators lay in the emphasis Islamic criminal law placed on private retribution. Because homicide was categorized as a private civil injury (_kisas_), the sovereign state did not act as the primary prosecutor. The right to initiate legal proceedings, determine whether a murderer should be executed or fined via _diya_, or grant an absolute pardon resided exclusively with the victim’s legal heirs. If a murdered individual possessed no surviving heirs, or if the heirs were minors, prosecution stalled indefinitely. To British officials trained in English jurisprudence—where major crimes were prosecuted as offenses against the Crown—this system appeared unpredictable, unconcerned with public deterrence, and vulnerable to private corruption.

To modify this framework without openly discarding Islamic law, EIC administrators leveraged an established doctrine within Sunni legal theory: the concept of _**Seeasut**_ (_**Siyasat**_). Within Hanafi jurisprudence, _seeasut_ represented the discretionary coercive authority vested in the ruler or executive sovereign to administer supplementary punishment, enact administrative regulations, and enforce summary justice for the public good (_maslaha_). While _hadd_ and _kisas_ protected private and divine rights, _seeasut_ empowered the state to intervene when private legal remedies failed to maintain public order.

Company administrators seized upon _seeasut_ as their legal justification. By asserting that the EIC, in its capacity as _Diwan_ and effective sovereign, was exercising legitimate _seeasut_ authority, the Bengal Government began modifying Islamic criminal procedure through Company Regulations. This approach allowed officials to circumvent restrictive Hanafi evidentiary rules, alter sentencing frameworks, and override private pardons while maintaining that they were operating within the broader boundaries of Islamic jurisprudence.

This application of _seeasut_ provided the philosophical justification for the drastic legislative interventions enacted under Governor-General Lord Cornwallis between 1790 and 1793.

### 3. The Cornwallis Code and the Construction of Legal Fictions (1790–1793)

In November 1790, Governor-General Lord Cornwallis executed an operational shift in Company governance by stripping the Naib Nazim (the Nawab’s chief deputy, Reza Khan) of criminal judicial authority and assuming direct control over the _Nizamut_ (criminal justice administration). Citing the "shocking abuses and wretched administration of justice in the Foujdary department," Cornwallis replaced native criminal tribunals with four provincial Courts of Circuit presided over by European Company servants, supported by native _maulvis_. To realign Islamic criminal jurisprudence with British concepts of public justice, Cornwallis initiated a series of systematic legislative interventions, codified within the landmark Bengal Regulations of 1793 (the Cornwallis Code).

#### 3.1 Redefining Homicide: Intentionality over Instrumentality

Under traditional Hanafi doctrine, the legal definition of willful murder (_katl-amd_) turned primarily on the physical instrument used to commit the crime. Abu Hanifa, the founder of the Hanafi school, held that intentional homicide could only be proved if the killing was committed with a lethal weapon—such as a sword, spear, gun, or sharp cutting instrument. If a victim was killed using a non-lethal instrument—such as a heavy wooden club, a stone, fire, or by being thrown into water—the crime was classified as quasi-deliberate homicide (_katl-shibh-amd_). For these non-instrumental killings, _kisas_ (capital punishment) was strictly barred, and the court could only award _diya_ (blood money payable by the killer's family).

Cornwallis overturned this distinction through Section 33 of the Bengal Judicial Regulation of 3 December 1790. This regulation removed the physical nature of the weapon as the defining test for murder, substituting the subjective criminal intent of the perpetrator as the sole criterion for capital homicide:

"That the intention of the party accused, rather than the form or species of the instrument used, shall in future determine the crime of willful murder; and that if it shall appear... that the party accused did willfully and maliciously kill... such party shall be deemed guilty of willful murder, notwithstanding the instrument used... was not an instrument usually calculated to cause death."

To maintain statutory legitimacy and avoid accusations of imposing foreign Christian laws, Cornwallis cited classical Islamic jurisprudence to justify this reform. He relied on the opinions of Abu Hanifa’s two prominent disciples, Abu Yusuf and Mahomed ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani. Unlike their master, Yusuf and Mahomed argued that any instrument inherently capable of causing death could demonstrate lethal intent. By citing Yusuf and Mahomed against Abu Hanifa, Cornwallis framed his modification of homicide law not as an English imposition, but as a legitimate selection among competing classical Islamic legal authorities.

#### 3.2 Subverting Private Prosecution through Legal Fictions

Having redefined homicide based on intent, the Cornwallis administration addressed the procedural rule that granted victims' next of kin the right to pardon murderers or commute capital sentences to blood money (_diya_). Section 34 of the Judicial Regulation of 3 December 1790 and the subsequent Regulation of 13 April 1792 stripped heirs of these discretionary powers. The state assumed exclusive control over capital prosecutions, decreeing that a murderer’s sentence would be determined by statutory law regardless of whether the heirs pardoned the offender, accepted _diya_, or refused to prosecute.

To enforce these substantive changes without formally abolishing the native law officers' role, the Company created a procedural mechanism known as the **"Dual-Futwa" legal fiction**, formally consolidated in Bengal Regulation IX of 1793. This legal fiction operated through a structured, step-by-step procedural process:

1. **Initial Trial and First Futwa:** The trial for a major crime was conducted before a European Circuit Judge, with native law officers (_kazis_ or _maulvis_) sitting alongside to evaluate the evidence. At the conclusion of testimony, the law officer was required to issue an initial _fatwa_ declaring whether the accused was guilty under standard Hanafi rules.
2. **Identification of Procedural Obstacles:** If the law officer's first _fatwa_ barred a conviction or capital sentence due to a classical Hanafi procedural rule—such as non-prosecution by the victim's heirs, an inadmissible non-Muslim witness, or the use of a non-lethal instrument—the European judge did not immediately overturn the ruling.
3. **Requirement of the Hypothetical Second Futwa:** Instead, the European judge directed the law officer to deliver a hypothetical second _fatwa_. The judge instructed the law officer to assume specific legal fictions:
   - Assume that the victim's next of kin had formally brought a prosecution for _kisas_,
   - Assume that a non-Muslim witness was a qualified Muslim male, or
   - Assume that the crime was committed with a recognized lethal instrument.
4. **Mandatory Referral and Sentence Execution:** Upon receiving this hypothetical second _fatwa_ declaring that the prisoner _would_ have been liable to capital punishment had the legal friction not existed, the local trial judge suspended sentencing. The complete trial transcript was forwarded directly to the _Sudder Nizamut Adawlut_ (the Company’s supreme criminal court in Calcutta) for final review, where European supreme judges issued the binding sentence of capital punishment or life imprisonment, completely overriding the initial Hanafi procedural bar.

The legal fictions established under Cornwallis laid the groundwork for the complete judicial neutralization of Muslim law officers in the early 19th century.

### 4. Executive Overrides and the Progressive Dissolution of Islamic Law (1797–1832)

During the first three decades of the nineteenth century, the East India Company shifted from modifying Islamic criminal law through legal fictions to directly overriding and ultimately marginalizing Muslim court law officers (_maulvis_ and _kazis_). As European administrators grew confident in their judicial authority, the statutory framework increasingly prioritized central state police power over traditional Islamic jurisprudence.

#### 4.1 Statutory Commutations and the Abolition of Privileges

The systematic modification of substantive criminal law accelerated through progressive statutory commutations. Physical mutilation—a primary _hadd_ penalty for aggravated theft and highway robbery—was abolished by executive order on 10 October 1791 and codified in Section 51 of Bengal Regulation IX of 1793. Courts were ordered to commute limb amputations to terms of hard labour in prison, setting seven years of hard labour for the loss of a single limb and fourteen years for the loss of two limbs. Similarly, Section 56 of Bengal Regulation IX of 1793 incorporated the Regulation of 27 April 1792, completely removing religious affiliation as a bar to witness competence in criminal trials.

Concurrently, Company regulations moved toward establishing formal legal equality by dismantling high-caste privileges within the criminal justice framework. In 1795, Resident Jonathan Duncan had persuaded Governor-General Cornwallis to grant special legal exemptions to Benares Brahmins under Section 23 of Bengal Regulation XVI of 1795 and Sections 7 and 9 of Bengal Regulation XXI of 1795. Under these provisions, a Brahmin convicted of murder in the sacred province of Benares could not be subjected to capital punishment; their sentence was automatically commuted to transportation or life imprisonment to prevent the spiritual guilt associated with Brahminicide.

This exemption drew increasing criticism from Company administrators as an impediment to public justice. Bengal Regulation XVII of 1817 (Section 15) formally rescinded the Brahmin legal immunities granted in 1795. Section 15 instructed criminal courts to execute death sentences against convicted Brahmins in Benares in the same manner as any other subject, provided only that the execution did not take place on a spot of ground held sacred by Hindus. This reform served as an important move toward formal legal equality in Company criminal procedure, arriving nearly a decade before Wynn’s Jury Act of 1826 (7 Geo IV c. 37) admitted non-Christian Indians to sit on petty juries in presidency towns.

#### 4.2 The Judicial Eclipse of the _Futwa_

The procedural fiction of relying on _fatwas_ while routinely overriding them reached a turning point between 1817 and 1832. Bengal Regulation XVII of 1817 (Section 4) formally authorized the judges of the _Sudder Nizamut Adawlut_ to override a law officer's _fatwa_ of acquittal. If the European judges were satisfied that a prisoner was guilty based on the trial record, they were empowered to convict and sentence the accused regardless of an acquittal issued by the native law officers. Five years later, Bengal Regulation IV of 1822 (Sections 1–2) expanded this executive override power by authorizing _Sudder Nizamut Adawlut_ judges to override a _fatwa_ of conviction if they determined the prisoner was innocent or deserving of mercy.

The procedural marginalization of Muslim criminal jurisprudence was completed with the enactment of **Bengal Regulation VI of 1832**. Section 6 of Regulation VI of 1832 authorized European trial judges to dispense with calling for a _fatwa_ altogether. The regulation permitted European judges presiding over criminal trials to choose from three alternative advisory frameworks:

1. Appointing native assessors or panchayats to assist the court,
2. Empaneling an advisory jury (though empanelment was rarely utilized in mofussil practice), or
3. Conducting the trial independently without consulting native law officers or advisory bodies.

Regulation VI of 1832 marked the effective end of Islamic criminal law as the operational law of the land in Bengal. While court law officers (_maulvis_ and _kazis_) retained a nominal presence in courtrooms for another three decades, their substantive authority was broken. European judges exercised unchecked discretion over criminal trials, relying on Company Regulations, judicial precedents, and the broad formula of "justice, equity and good conscience."

The procedural dismantling of the _fatwa_ enabled the Company to create extraordinary legal regimes to combat organized rural crimes like _thagi_ and dacoity.

### 5. Extraordinary Jurisprudence: The Campaigns against _Thagi_ and Dacoity (1803–1848)

In the early nineteenth century, the East India Company encountered forms of organized gang crime that challenged standard criminal justice procedures. Professional stranglers (_thugs_) and violent gang robbers (_dacoits_) operated across internal borders, utilized elaborate disguises, and intimidated local witnesses. In response, the EIC created an emergency legal framework. Shifting the focus of criminal law from proving specific, localized acts toward establishing membership in designated criminal categories, the Company introduced extraordinary procedural rules that bypassed ordinary court protections.

#### 5.1 Statutory Evolution of Gang Crimes

The statutory regulation of gang crime began with Bengal Regulation LIII of 1803 (duplicated as Madras Regulation XV of 1803). Designed to circumvent Hanafi restrictions on highway robbery, Section 3 of Regulation LIII defined the broad statutory offense of "robbery by open violence" (_suruca-i-kubra_, commonly known as _shubkhoone_ or _dukaytee_). It declared that any individuals who formed a gang armed with offensive weapons to rob or attempt to rob were guilty of gang robbery, subjecting leaders and members to capital punishment or transportation for life.

When gang robbery continued to increase, the Bengal Government enacted Regulation IX of 1808 (duplicated as Madras Regulation XIII of 1809). Regulation IX introduced extraordinary police powers designed to apprehend _sirdars_ (leaders) of dacoit gangs. It authorized magistrates to publicly proclaim suspected dacoits by printed notice and beat of drum. If a proclaimed suspect failed to surrender within two months, they were automatically judged guilty of gang robbery without trial and sentenced to life imprisonment or transportation. Furthermore, Section 11 of the regulation declared that any private citizen or police officer who wounded or killed a proclaimed dacoit while attempting to apprehend them was completely guiltless under the law.

By the late 1820s, official concern shifted toward _thagi_—habitual, professional stranglers operating along high roads in central and northern India. Sleeman, Sherwood, and J.S. Mill detailed a distinct typology of these marauders:

- _**Phansigars**_ (stranglers using a noose or rope, derived from _phansi_);
- _**Megpunnas**_ (child-stealers who murdered parents to sell children into slavery);
- _**Dhutooreas**_ or _**Meetawallas**_ (poisoners who incapacitated or killed victims using datura seeds); and
- _**Ari Tulucar**_ or _**Tanti Calleru**_ (regional Tamil and Canarese designations for Muslim noosers and thieves using wire or catgut nooses).

In 1829, Governor-General Lord William Bentinck established the specialized Thagi Department under the direction of F.C. Smith (Agent to the Governor-General in the Saugor and Nerbudda Territories) and Captain William H. Sleeman. To support this centralized department, the Legislative Council of India enacted **Act XXX of 1836**. Act XXX revolutionized criminal procedure by establishing penal liability based solely on gang membership:

"Whoever shall be proved to have belonged, either before or after the passing of this Act, to any gang of Thugs, either within or without the Territories of the East India Company, shall be punished with imprisonment for life with hard labour."

Act XXX eliminated established procedural safeguards:

- It criminalized mere association with a gang, eliminating the requirement to prove a specific act of murder or robbery.
- Section 2 established extraordinary cross-presidency jurisdiction, allowing an accused Thug to be tried by any Company tribunal in India regardless of where the alleged crime occurred or where the suspect was apprehended.
- Section 3 formally dispensed with calling for a _fatwa_ from Muslim law officers in all Thuggee trials.

This extraordinary statutory regime was subsequently expanded. Act XXIV of 1843 extended these provisions to gang robbery, penalizing any person proved to have "belonged to any gang of Dacoits" with life transportation or hard-labour imprisonment. Act III of 1848 resolved legal doubts by expanding the definition of a Thug to include poisoners (_dhutooreas_ or _meetawallas_), defining a Thug as any person habitually associated with others to commit robbery or child-stealing by lethal means. Finally, Act XI of 1848 extended these procedures to "wandering gangs of persons associated for the purposes of theft or robbery."

#### 5.2 The Approver System, Legal Exceptions, and Macaulay’s Intervention

The operational success of Sleeman’s Thagi Department relied upon the **Approver System**. Because Thugs operated secretly and left no living eyewitnesses, standard judicial evidence was rarely available. Sleeman and F.C. Smith addressed this by offering captured Thugs conditional or partial pardons. In exchange for a complete confession detailing their criminal careers and testifying against their accomplices in court, a captured Thug was spared execution or overseas transportation. Instead, the "approver" was sentenced to life confinement within specialized reformatory institutions—most notably the **Jubbulpore School of Industry** (established in 1838). There, captured thugs and their children were trained in industrial crafts (such as carpet weaving and tent making), showcasing a Benthamite utilitarian shift toward reformatory confinement noted approvingly by J.S. Mill.

The approver system encountered significant legal challenges in the courts. European trial judges routinely questioned the credibility of confessed murderers testifying against former associates, while existing criminal regulations required a full executive pardon before an accomplice could give admissible evidence.

Law Member Thomas Babington Macaulay addressed this procedural obstacle through **Act XIX of 1837**. Applying Benthamite principles of utility and evidence, Macaulay rejected the idea of creating a separate, exceptional code of evidence exclusively for Thuggee trials. Instead, Act XIX established a universal rule of evidence across all criminal jurisprudence in British India:

> "No person shall, by reason of any conviction for any offence whatever, be incompetent to be a witness in any stage of any Cause or Matter, Civil or Criminal, in any Court..."

Act XIX established that a prior criminal conviction or confessed participation in a capital crime affected only the _weight_ or _credibility_ of a witness's testimony, not their legal _competency_ to testify. This reform validated the testimony of convicted Thug approvers, allowing the Thagi Department to secure convictions based on approver testimony corroborated by circumstantial evidence.

A crucial distinction must be drawn between procedural commitments under Act XVIII of 1837 and cross-border prisoner detention under **Act XVIII of 1843**. While Act XVIII of 1837 authorized magisterial officers of the Thagi Department to commit accused persons directly to trial before designated courts, Act XVIII of 1843 ("Better Custody of Persons Convicted of Thuggee and Dacoity") resolved complex cross-border jurisdictional dilemmas. It authorized Company jails within EIC territories to receive and detain criminals convicted and sentenced in Princely or Native States (such as Gwalior), even when the offender was not a British subject and had committed no crime within Company territory.

### 6. Regional Legal Pluralism and the Boundaries of Social Control

The transformation of criminal jurisprudence did not proceed uniformly across British India. Significant regional variations separated the Bengal, Madras, and Bombay Presidencies. While Bengal and Madras relied on piecemeal Company Regulations that modified Islamic criminal law, the Bombay Presidency pursued codification decades earlier, establishing a distinct statutory framework.

#### 6.1 The Elphinstone Code vs. Regulation Presidencies

Under Governor Mountstuart Elphinstone, the Bombay Presidency established a distinct legal framework codified within the **Bombay Code of 1827** (specifically Regulation XIV of 1827, known as the **Elphinstone Code**). Elphinstone explicitly rejected the Bengal model of using modified Islamic criminal law as the general criminal law for all inhabitants. Citing the injustice of subjecting Hindu populations to modified _Sharia_, the Bombay Code reserved Hindu criminal law for Hindus, Muslim criminal law for Muslims, and established the "law of the defendant" as a governing principle.

Furthermore, Regulation XIV of 1827 constructed a self-contained statutory penal code that classified crimes and apportioned punishments independently of _Sharia_. The Elphinstone Code explicitly protected local custom and usage:

- Section 26 of Bombay Regulation IV of 1827 declared that local usage and custom took precedence over written religious law.
- Section 21 of Bombay Regulation II of 1827 excluded pure caste questions from the jurisdiction of civil courts.

This distinct legal tradition produced institutional conflict between the Bombay _Sudder Foujdary Adawlut_ and the Supreme Government in Calcutta over the extraordinary Thuggee acts (Act XXX of 1836 and Act XVIII of 1837). Senior Bombay judges—including P.W. LeGeyt—strongly opposed these acts. They argued that Bombay Criminal Law was comprehensive, humane, and did not acknowledge Islamic criminal law in evidence or punishment.

The Bombay judges objected to Section 2 of Act XXX of 1836, which authorized sending Bombay residents for trial before distant tribunals in central India, arguing it violated Chapter I of Bombay Regulation XI of 1827 (designed to protect Bombay subjects from foreign executive overreach). Furthermore, the judges criticized the Thagi Department's approver system, pointing out that Clause 1, Section 35 of Bombay Regulation XII of 1827 barred convictions based solely on uncorroborated accomplice testimony. They argued that relying on accomplice testimony without independent corroboration invited perjury, false accusations, and extortion. Although the Supreme Government ultimately overruled these objections—repealing the conflicting Bombay clause via Act XV of 1838—the conflict underscored the deep institutional division between Bombay’s statutory protections and Bengal’s centralized executive administration.

#### 6.2 Sorcery, Superstition, and the Limits of Statutory Intervention

The boundaries of Company legal intervention were regularly tested by cases involving sorcery, witch-killings, and popular superstitious practices. In primitive or isolated rural regions—such as Chota Nagpur in Ramgurh district, the Northern Konkan, and Malabar—belief in witchcraft (_dakin_) was widespread. When epidemics or droughts occurred, village assemblies conducted trials by ordeal to identify alleged witches.

In the 1792 Ramgurh trial of three Sutar caste members who killed five alleged witches, the source context documents **three distinct ordeals** employed by the villagers:

1. Planting Saul tree branches marked with the names of all village women over age twelve in water for 4.5 hours (withering branches proved guilt);
2. Wrapping portions of rice in cloths marked with names and placing them in a white ants' nest (ants consuming the rice proved guilt); and
3. Performing a night ceremony with lighted lamps and water in leaf cups, dripping mustard seed and oil into the water while reciting names to observe falling shadows on the water.

The Company’s initial legal response occurred under Sir John Shore via **Section 6 of Bengal Regulation IV of 1797**, which established that a belief in sorcery could never justify homicide or mitigate a murder charge. This principle was incorporated into Madras Regulation VII of 1802 (Section 34) and Bombay Regulation VIII of 1812 (Section 18), before being refined in the Elphinstone Code (Clause 2, Section 26 of Bombay Regulation XIV of 1827) into a concise rule: the belief that sorcery was practiced by a deceased person was inadmissible as a justification for homicide.

Fears of sorcery frequently intersected with low-caste subjection. In his 1834–1836 judicial minutes, Circuit Judge A.D. Campbell of Madras highlighted trials on the Malabar Coast and in the Nilgiri Hills involving agricultural slave castes. Campbell documented intense high-caste fears that low-caste _Pariar_ (Pariah) slaves possessed magical powers to inflict fatal illness or "conjure the living fetus from the mother's womb." Campbell cited an 1834 trial where a Crab Tree nut shell was found in a deceased woman's womb, an 1829 trial involving two slaves accused of inserting a calf skin in an earthen pot into a corpse, and an 1834 massacre in the Nilgiri Hills where a mob bound fifteen _Pariar_ slaves, submerged them in a river to near-suffocation, forced their children to rub sand into their wounds, and expelled them after destroying their homes.

Appalled by these atrocities, Campbell urged the Government to enact a statute directly criminalizing the _practice_ of sorcery. Macaulay’s Law Commission firmly rejected Campbell’s proposal in 1837. Applying Benthamite logic, the Commission argued that enacting a law against practicing sorcery would backfire: in a society where belief in magic was widespread, penalizing the art would lead the public to conclude that the law itself acknowledged the reality of sorcery, thereby reinforcing superstitious belief.

Similarly, when the Madras _Sudder Foujdary Adawlut_ proposed a regulation in 1835 authorizing police to strip and confiscate gold and silver ornaments from children found wandering alone (to prevent children from being murdered for their jewelry), the Law Commission and Governor-General Lord Auckland rejected the measure in 1838. The Commission reasoned that parental pride in decorating children was a positive social incentive that encouraged industry and civil commerce. Furthermore, judicial consultations across India indicated that such a law would cause widespread public disaffection and expose poor families to police extortion. Instead, the Government chose to rely on public warnings—reissuing the Bengal _Nizamut Adawlut_ Circular Order of 4 February 1818—to advise parents to protect their decorated children, keeping the formal criminal law focused strictly on violent acts rather than private domestic habits.

These regional experiments and legislative boundaries connected directly to the ultimate movement toward all-India legal codification.

### 7. Codification, Law Commissions, and the Legacy of Anglo-Indian Penal Law

By the 1830s, the piecemeal system of regional Company Regulations—characterized by conflicting presidency laws, complex procedural fictions, and broad executive overrides—had grown overly unwieldy. The drive for legal reform shifted from ad-hoc executive interventions toward formal all-India codification, guided by British Utilitarian philosophy.

#### 7.1 The Benthamite Impulse and the Charter Act of 1833

The philosophical foundation for all-India codification derived directly from Jeremy Bentham's Utilitarian theories of jurisprudence, championed in colonial administration by James Mill and Thomas Babington Macaulay. The statutory mechanism for this reform was Section 53 of the **Charter Act of 1833** (3 & 4 Will IV c. 85). Section 53 authorized the creation of a centralized legislative authority for British India, created the office of Fourth Legal Member of the Governor-General’s Council, and instituted the First Indian Law Commission.

Appointed as the first Legal Member in 1834, Macaulay assumed leadership of the First Law Commission. In a speech before the House of Commons during the debate on the 1833 Charter, Macaulay articulated the governing philosophy of Anglo-Indian codification:

"Our principle is simply this—uniformity where you can have it—diversity where you must have it—but in all cases certainty."

Macaulay and his fellow Law Commissioners set out to draft a universal penal code for British India. Discarding existing regional regulations, English common law technicalities, and Islamic penal provisions, Macaulay produced the **Draft Penal Code of 1837**. The draft code established a rationalized schedule of offenses and punishments, organized around precise legal definitions, systematic illustrations, and universal applicability across all subjects regardless of religion or caste.

Concurrently, the Law Commission addressed the boundaries of codification. While early reformers had envisioned codifying all Hindu and Muslim laws, the **Second Law Commission (1855)** explicitly abandoned this goal. The 1855 Report concluded that "no portion either of the Mahomedan Law or of the Hindu Law ought to be enacted as such in any form by a British Legislature." This structural retreat from codifying substantive Hindu or Muslim personal laws established a permanent division in modern Indian jurisprudence. By confining uniform all-India codification strictly to public criminal law while preserving religious personal laws (inheritance, marriage, succession) in civil courts, the British Raj was able to project an overarching "unitary state" while insulating local domestic religious spheres, thereby avoiding widespread public disaffection post-1857.

#### 7.2 The Historical Synthesis: From Regulations to the Penal Code of 1860

The work of the early Law Commissions culminated following the political crisis of 1857–1858, when the British Crown assumed direct governance over India from the East India Company. Under the direction of Sir Barnes Peacock (Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Calcutta), Macaulay’s 1837 draft code was updated and formally enacted into law as the **Indian Penal Code (Act XLV of 1860)**, taking effect on 1 January 1862.

The Indian Penal Code institutionalized the cumulative modifications made to Islamic jurisprudence during the Company era:

- **Subjective Intent in Homicide:** Sections 299–304 codified Cornwallis’s 1790 rule, establishing subjective intent (_mens rea_) rather than the instrument used as the criterion for culpable homicide and murder.
- **Criminalization of Gang Membership:** Sections 310–311 institutionalized the Thuggee acts, penalizing anyone associated with a gang for the purpose of committing robbery or child-stealing accompanied by murder with mandatory life imprisonment. Sections 391–402 codified the Dacoity regulations, penalizing gang robbery committed by five or more persons.
- **Elimination of Personal Privileges & Procedural Bars:** Religious restrictions on witness competency, high-caste immunities, and private victim pardons were completely replaced by uniform public state prosecutions.

The institutional transformation was completed with the enactment of **Act XI of 1864**, which formally abolished the offices of Hindu _pandits_ and Muslim _kazis_ or _maulvis_ attached to Company courts. Native law officers were no longer required to deliver legal opinions, as European judges now administered the written, codified statutes of British India directly.

Anglo-Indian criminal law was not a mere imposition of English common law, but an ad-hoc, highly engineered hybrid built upon the adapted chassis of modified Islamic jurisprudence. Through decades of Company Regulations, legal fictions, executive overrides, and Utilitarian codification, East India Company administrators systematically transformed a decentralized Islamic penal system rooted in private restitution into a centralized, state-monopolized public justice regime.
