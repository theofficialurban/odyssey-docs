Through a detailed analysis of news discourse, marketing, popular culture, and independent reporting, the book deconstructs the **discursive struggle** between dominant military narratives that portray drones as precise and virtuous, and alternative perspectives that highlight the terror and civilian casualties of remote-controlled warfare. ## **Conceptual Learning Module: Framing the Flying Machine – Media Discourse, the Technological Sublime, and the Social Construction of Drones** {#primer}

### Section 1: Module Overview & Foundational Framework

#### 1.1 Purpose and Learning Objectives

Welcome to this conceptual learning module. As an aspiring scholar of media ecology, critical discourse analysis, and cultural studies, you are about to step into an investigation of how language, media narratives, and symbolic representations actively shape technological development, legal parameters, and societal acceptance. Technology is never merely a collection of neutral wires, microprocessors, and composite metals; it is thoroughly cultural from its inception. The stories, metaphors, and visual frames we construct around a physical artifact dictate whether a culture embraces it as an instrument of democratic progress or dreads it as a tool of authoritarian surveillance and extrajudicial violence. By deconstructing media representations of technological systems, we uncover the hidden power dynamics and orders of discourse that determine how modern sociotechnical artifacts are governed, normalized, and integrated into everyday life.

- **Analyze** how competing linguistic framings and media discourses socially construct technological artifacts, establishing parameters for public policy, ethical boundaries, and executive power.

- **Apply** the theoretical concepts of the "technological sublime," "anticipatory discourse," and "distributed intimacy" to evaluate historical antecedents and contemporary media representations of emerging sociotechnical systems.

### Section 2: The Power of Naming – Language as Reality Construction

#### 2.1 The Battles Over Linguistic Framing ("Don't Call Them Drones")

Language is never a transparent window through which we view an objective reality; rather, word selection establishes the precise parameters of human perception, emotional resonance, and public policy. The fierce conflict surrounding the terminology used for unmanned aerial vehicles illustrates how competing societal groups deploy deliberate linguistic strategies to manage public perception, navigate cultural anxieties, and assert institutional authority.

- **Military Leadership:** High-ranking military officials vehemently reject the term "drone," viewing it as a reductive, pejorative moniker that implies an unthinking, automated, and indiscriminate instrument of destruction. In an August 2012 interview with NPR reporter Tom Bowman, outgoing US Air Force General Norton Schwartz explicitly corrected his interviewer: _"Drones mischaracterize what these things are... They're not dumb. Nor are they unmanned, actually. They're remotely piloted aircraft."_ Similarly, in May 2014, returning from a NATO defense meeting in Brussels, US Army General Martin Dempsey stated to reporters: _"You will never hear me use the word drone... They are remotely piloted aircraft."_ Defense leadership insists upon the designation **Remotely Piloted Aircraft (RPA)** because it foregrounds human agency, emphasizing the presence of highly trained military pilots, operational discipline, and tactical precision.
- **Industry Entrepreneurs & Trade Organizations:** Commercial developers, corporate investors, and trade groups view the word "drone" as a severe economic liability that evokes terrifying media images of overseas military strikes. Ray Mann, owner of a civilian airport for unmanned aircraft, told the _Sunday Times_: _"Don't call them drones... It makes people fearful. It has a sinister sound to it."_ Lucien Miller, CEO of Innov-8tive Designs, similarly noted the public relations challenge created by daily news reports of _"Predators and their Hellfire missiles bombing daycare centers."_ To protect the commercial market, the industry's primary trade organization, the Association for Unmanned Vehicle Systems International (AUVSI), launched aggressive campaigns instructing journalists to use terms like **Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS)** or **Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV)**. To enforce this semiotic boundary, reporters entering the press room at the 2013 AUVSI national convention in Washington, DC, discovered that the public Wi-Fi password was explicitly configured as `DONTSAYDRONE`.
- **Public & Journalists:** For news reporters and the general public, "drone" remains the intuitive, universally recognized term. However, as technology journalist Joe Pappalardo observed following an encounter with a veteran police officer who threatened to shoot down any flying machine near his boat, the term carries deep military and law enforcement connotations. These associations evoke latent public anxieties over personal safety, state surveillance, algorithmic error, and the loss of human control.

#### 2.2 Theoretical Grounding: Language Constituting "The Real"

Drawing upon discourse theory—rooted in media theorist James Carey's communication model and philosopher Michel Foucault's analysis of discursive formations—language does not merely reflect an objective, pre-existing reality; it actively constitutes "the real." Symbolic forms structure human access to reality. Because technological meaning is not hardwired into physical components, an unmanned aircraft acquires fundamentally different social meanings depending upon the cultural and political matrix within which it is framed.

#### 2.3 Connective Tissue Bridge

### Section 3: Analytical Method – Fairclough's Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA)

#### 3.1 The Three-Dimensional Model

To systematically investigate how media texts construct technological artifacts, reinforce institutional dominance, and naturalize specific political ideologies, Critical Discourse Analysis employs Norman Fairclough’s tripartite framework. This method requires learners to analyze media along three interdependent dimensions:

#### Fairclough’s Tripartite Analytical Framework

1. **Text (Formal Features):** Micro-level analysis focusing on formal linguistic, visual, and auditory properties. Key textual features include:
   - _Wording and Metaphor:_ Specific vocabulary choices designed to evoke target emotional or ideological associations.
   - _Collocations:_ Words repeatedly paired together to construct implicit conceptual links (e.g., the routine news pairing of "Islamic" with "militant" or "extremist").
   - _Nominalization:_ The grammatical transformation of verbs (actions) into nouns (events). For example, converting "an operator dropped a bomb and killed civilians" into the nominal phrase "a drone strike occurred" removes active human agency, turning a deliberate action into an agentless event and obscuring moral responsibility.
2. **Discursive Practice (Production, Circulation & Consumption):** Meso-level analysis examining how texts are produced by institutional actors, mediated through technology, and interpreted by audiences. Key concepts include:
   - _Intertextuality:_ The explicit or implicit ways a current text references, quotes, or builds upon previous texts.
   - _Interdiscursivity:_ The blending of distinct discourses, registers, or genres within a single text (e.g., combining objective news reporting with science-fiction tropes or corporate promotional copy).
   - _Intertextual Chains:_ The process through which a statement or media artifact is extracted, transformed, and recirculated across multiple platforms over time.
3. **Social Practice (Broader Context & Structural Power):** Macro-level analysis examining the non-discursive political, economic, and institutional forces shaping the text—what Fairclough terms the "social matrix of discourse." This dimension analyzes corporate media ownership, advertising dependencies, political economy, state power, and the broader hegemonic _orders of discourse_.

#### 3.2 Discursive Practice in Action: Deconstructing the Audi "The Drones" Ad

A practical demonstration of Fairclough’s second dimension—discursive practice—is provided by luxury automaker Audi’s high-profile 2015 television commercial for the A6 sedan, titled "The Drones."

##### Intertextuality

The commercial operates as a direct cinematic homage to Alfred Hitchcock’s 1963 horror classic _The Birds_. In the spot, office workers exiting a sleek corporate building are terrorized by a menacing swarm of parcel-delivering quadcopters. The commercial directly mirrors Hitchcockian staging: a panicked executive flees inside a glass parking booth, only for an aggressive delivery drone to smash violently against the glass window in single-minded pursuit of its human target.

##### Interdiscursivity

Audi skillfully blends the promotional register of commercial advertising with the suspenseful, uncanny discourse of cinematic horror. By deploying irony, spectacle, and horror tropes, the spot taps into ambient consumer anxieties regarding technological intrusion and automated chaos. It then resolves that anxiety through industrial engineering: the Audi A6's advanced dashboard technology, soundproof cabin, and responsive handling allow the driver to easily outmaneuver the mechanical swarm. The ad concludes with the reassuring tagline: _"Technology doesn't have to be intimidating."_

##### Intertextual Chains

The commercial forms a link in an intertextual chain that originated with Amazon CEO Jeff Bezos’s December 2013 appearance on CBS’s _60 Minutes_, where he announced Amazon's "Prime Air" parcel-delivery concept. Audi's commercial relies upon the viewer's prior exposure to Amazon's promotional broadcast and the subsequent wave of satirical internet memes, transforming Amazon's high-tech promise into a satirical horror scenario to position its automobile as a sanctuary of human control.

### Section 4: The Technological Sublime – Between Wonder and Terror

#### 4.1 Defining the Sublime: Dualities of Awe and Fear

Coined in cultural theory by Leo Marx in _The Machine in the Garden_ and expanded by David Nye in _American Technological Sublime_, the **technological sublime** denotes a cultural trope wherein new human technological achievements evoke emotional reactions historically reserved for majestic, terrifying natural landscapes—a mixture of awe, reverent wonder, and deep dread. In nineteenth-century America, technological development shifted away from Jeffersonian pastoralism toward an "official American ideology of industrialism." Within this paradigm, machines were interpreted not merely as mechanical tools, but as physical and symbolic manifestations of Manifest Destiny and the "moral machine"—promising national unity, economic prosperity, and the triumph of human intellect over nature.

"The technological sublime is characterized by repeated experiences of awe and wonder, often tinged with an element of terror... It taps into fundamental hopes and fears, functioning as a preferred trope in American cultural history to articulate a distinct social and political relation to technology."

This duality creates a psychological state of "negative lust"—a simultaneous pull of attraction and repulsion:

- **Hopes (The Virtuous Machine):** The artifact is discursively constructed as an engine of moral progress, national identity, and human liberation. In the nineteenth century, the railroad and telegraph were celebrated for the "annihilation of space and time." Modern drones are similarly framed by military and industry advocates as virtuous tools that perform "dull, dirty, or dangerous" work, eliminating human risk and conducting surgical, bloodless operations.
- **Fears (The Menacing Machine):** The artifact is discursively constructed as an unmanageable specter of dehumanization, tyranny, and destruction. Where nineteenth-century critics feared that "men will become machines," contemporary discourse manifests apocalyptic anxieties regarding autonomous "killerbots," omnipresent state surveillance, and unannounced death raining down from remote skies.

#### 4.2 Anticipatory Discourse and Deep Cultural Antecedents

Technologies do not emerge into a vacuum. Long before physical hardware matures, society engages in **anticipatory discourse**—narrative practices that project, imagine, and stake claims on the future, legitimating research, capital allocation, and political deployment. Modern drones possess deep cultural antecedents across three distinct historical realms:

- **Ancient Myth:**
  - _Avian Monsters:_ Terrifying winged creatures delivering sudden divine judgment, such as the Greek **Harpies** or the **Ababeel** birds described in the Quran (mythic birds that defended Mecca by dropping stones on invading armies—a term applied by Waziri tribesmen to modern US drones hovering overhead).
  - _Supernatural Weaponry:_ Myths of self-guided, autonomous tools of destruction, including Norse mythology's **Freyr's Sword** (a magical blade that fights on its own) and Thor's hammer **Mjölnir** (which never missed its target and returned to the owner), as well as the Sumerian **Sharur** (a sentient, flying mace that communicated with its wielder and gathered intelligence on enemy movements).
- **Tesla's Telautomatics and the "Borrowed Mind":**
  - At the 1898 Electrical Exhibition in New York, inventor **Nikola Tesla** demonstrated his radio-controlled boat, the "teleautomaton." In his seminal 1900 _Century Magazine_ essay, _"The Problem of Increasing Human Energy,"_ Tesla articulated the concept of **telautomatics**: controlling distant automatons through an operator's **"borrowed mind."** Tesla theorized that imbuing a distant machine with human judgment and reason would enable teleoperated weapons to fight machine-versus-machine wars, arguing that replacing human soldiers with artificial automatons would achieve universal peace. This conceptualization established the early scientific imaginary for teleoperation, directly anticipating contemporary debates regarding autonomous AI and human-in-the-loop robotic warfare.
- **Science Fiction:**
  - Sci-fi functions as an anticipatory narrative sandbox. George Orwell’s _1984_ envisioned ubiquitous aerial surveillance; Philip K. Dick’s _Vulcan's Hammer_ depicted mechanical, swarming "flying hammers" that pursue humans like vengeful wasps; and Ian Watson’s _Slow Birds_ (1985) accurately portended the psychological horror of living under hovering, silvery composite-metal aircraft that unpredictably appear, hover, and vanish.

#### 4.3 Connective Tissue Bridge

The emotional drivers of the technological sublime and the predictive power of anticipatory discourse are routinely mobilized by state actors during national crises to construct dominant geopolitical narratives and normalize wartime executive power.

### Section 5: Case Study I – Constructing the Narrative of Perpetual War

#### 5.1 Critical Discourse Moments in the Escalation of Drone Warfare

Sociologist William Gamson defines **critical discourse moments** as specific historical events that make discourse surrounding an issue exceptionally visible, stimulating intense commentary and forcing institutional actors to reframe public debate. The institutionalization of military drone strikes developed across four key critical discourse moments:

1. **September 1999 & December 2001 – George W. Bush’s Citadel Speeches (\*\***Discursive Framing Shift: Military Transformation\***\*):** In his September 1999 address "A Period of Consequences" at The Citadel, presidential candidate George W. Bush outlined a vision to "skip a generation" of conventional weapons by investing in futuristic, unmanned systems to "redefine war on our terms." Following 9/11, President Bush returned to The Citadel in December 2001, declaring that Afghanistan was a "proving ground" where armed Predators were "rewriting the rules of war." Media reporting adopted this framing, celebrating high-tech "revolution" while ignoring legal and strategic risks.
2. **November 2001 – The Mohammed Atef Strike (\*\***Discursive Framing Shift: The Lethal Tactical Paradigm\***\*):** In November 2001, a CIA Predator strike killed al-Qaeda military commander Mohammed Atef in Afghanistan. This strike marked the **first joint operation between an armed Predator and Navy jet fighters**, representing a major technological and tactical milestone that solidified the transition of UAVs from passive reconnaissance platforms to active, lethal strike platforms.
3. **February 4, 2002 – The Zhawar Kili "Tall Man" Strike (\*\***Discursive Framing Shift: Defensible Miscalculation\***\*):** A CIA Predator targeted a group in Zhawar Kili, Afghanistan, because operators spotted a "tall man" treated deferentially by others, suspecting Osama bin Laden. The Hellfire missile killed three innocent scrap-metal scavengers. When Afghan Deputy Border Affairs Minister Mirza Ali reported that the victims were starving local peasants, Pentagon spokesperson Rear Admiral John Stufflebeem (deputy operations director of the Joint Staff) defended the strike, claiming "something untoward" was occurring and presenting credit card applications and airline schedules found at the site as evidence of terrorism. Mainstream media adopted the frame of a "defensible miscalculation," deflecting scrutiny away from intelligence failures.
4. **November 4, 2002 – The Yemen al-Harethi Strike (\*\***Discursive Framing Shift: Normalizing "Targeted Killing"\***\*):** A CIA Predator incinerated a vehicle in Yemen carrying suspected al-Qaeda leader Qaed Salim Sinan al-Harethi and American citizen Kamal Derwish. Journalists proclaimed the attack the **"dawn of robotic warfare."** During this moment, US officials explicitly adopted the controversial term **"targeted killing"**—a concept previously pioneered by Israel and historically condemned by Washington. Through linguistic repetition, official spin successfully normalized state-sanctioned extrajudicial execution outside active war zones.

#### 5.2 Obama’s "Weapon of Choice" & Legal Elasticity

Under President Barack Obama, drone warfare expanded exponentially. President Obama authorized **283 strikes in Pakistan alone by late 2012** (compared to 44 to 50 strikes total during George W. Bush's eight-year presidency) and ordered over **500 strikes total between 2009 and 2015**. Drones became Obama's undisputed "weapon of choice." To insulate this covert campaign from legal challenge, the administration rebranded the bellicose "Global War on Terror" with technocratic euphemisms such as "Overseas Contingency Operations."

When a leaked 2013 Department of Justice (DOJ) White Paper exposed the legal framework used to execute US citizens (such as Anwar al-Awlaki) without trial, critical legal scholars highlighted how executive lawyers deployed "elastic definitions" to alter the plain meaning of established constitutional and international law:

| Term                                          | Official Distorted / Elastic Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Imminent Threat**                           | Redefined to **not** require specific evidence of an active, ongoing plot against the US homeland; rather, a general suspicion that an individual is a "senior operational leader" renders the threat perpetually "imminent," destroying the temporal requirement of immediate defense. |
| **Infeasible (Capture)**                      | Redefined to mean any scenario where capture operations would present an "undue risk" to US personnel or where a host nation is uncooperative, converting capture from a legal obligation into a matter of tactical convenience.                                                        |
| **High-Value Target / High-Ranking Official** | Redefined ambiguously to allow covert executive panels ("Terror Tuesdays") to place individuals on classified "kill lists" based on secret intelligence, completely bypassing judicial review and constitutional due process.                                                           |

#### 5.3 Dissension and Contestation: The Rand Paul Filibuster

In March 2013, a significant breakdown in elite political consensus occurred when Senator Rand Paul executed a 13-hour filibuster of John Brennan’s nomination for CIA Director.

- **Rupture of Consensus:** Paul challenged Attorney General Eric Holder’s assertion that the President possessed constitutional authority to execute Americans with drones on domestic soil under "extraordinary circumstances."
- **Intertextual Resistance:** Paul utilized intertextual references on the Senate floor, citing investigative journalism by Glenn Greenwald and Jeremy Scahill, while news media framed the spectacle through the classic Hollywood film _Mr. Smith Goes to Washington_.
- **Digital Activism:** The filibuster trended globally under the hashtag `#StandWithRand`, demonstrating how dissenting orders of discourse can disrupt official secrecy and force executive concessions (such as Holder's formal letter confirming that domestic drone execution of non-combatant citizens was unconstitutional).

### Section 6: Case Study II – The Discourse of Domestication

#### 6.1 Rebranding the Machine: Business, Agriculture, and Technological Analogy

To overcome public stigma associated with overseas military strikes, drone advocates, industry trade groups (AUVSI), and technology entrepreneurs (such as Chris Anderson) deployed a deliberate **discourse of domestication**. This strategy seeks to destigmatize the artifact by highlighting civilian utility (e.g., precision agriculture, ecological conservation, emergency delivery) and constructing persuasive technological analogies:

#### The 3 Core Domesticating Analogies

- **The Smartphone Analogy:** Frames the drone as a mobile device with propellers. By leveraging low-cost microprocessors, miniaturized sensors, and open-source software built for the cell phone industry, advocates argue: _"A drone is just a flying smartphone in your pocket."_
- **The Automobile Analogy:** Frames operational risk through societal utility. Advocates highlight that despite 33,000 annual US traffic fatalities, society accepts automobiles because of their immense utility; thus, drones deserve a similar framework of risk acceptance.
- **The Personal Computer Analogy:** Frames the drone as a dual-use platform. Advocates argue that just as personal computers can be misused for malware or identity theft, society does not ban the PC; rather, it regulates bad actors while liberating the tool for economic productivity.

#### 6.2 Popular Culture as a Cultural Barometer

Popular culture functions as a sensitive barometer, reflecting societal ambivalence toward unmanned systems by oscillating between technological awe and paranoid dread. In Chapter 9 of _Drones: Media Discourse and the Public Imagination_, Kevin Howley introduces the critical media ecology concept of **"distributed intimacy"**—the unidirectional, mediated closeness between remote drone operators and the targets they surveil for weeks via high-definition video feeds prior to striking. This concept explains the unique psychological trauma, moral disengagement, and voyeuristic proximity experienced by remote pilots.

### Section 7: Module Synthesis & Critical Takeaways

#### 7.1 Core Conceptual Takeaways

1. **Tools mirror their stories.** Technological artifacts are never purely physical objects; their physical design, legal governance, and social acceptance are fundamentally shaped by the discursive strategies constructed around them.
2. **Euphemisms hide power.** Strategic terminology ("RPA" vs. "UAS" vs. "Drone") and elastic legal definitions ("imminent threat," "infeasible") are semiotic tools deployed by institutions to insulate power from public debate and judicial accountability.
3. **The sublime drives adoption.** Emerging technologies inevitably provoke the technological sublime—a tension between transcendent hope (the virtuous machine) and existential fear (the menacing machine). Institutional actors manipulate the positive pole while deflecting the negative.
4. **Domestication masks violence.** Commercial rebranding utilizes familiar consumer analogies (smartphones, automobiles, personal computers) to normalize dual-use technologies, obscuring their origins in state surveillance and military violence.

## **Framing the Sky: A Strategic Media Discourse Analysis of Unmanned Aerial Technology** {#media-discourse}

### 1. Executive Context and Linguistic Framing Matrix

#### Context & Strategic Mandate

In technology policy communications and corporate public affairs, language is not a passive reflection of reality; it actively constructs reality. For strategic communications specialists navigating emerging, highly controversial dual-use technologies, linguistic choices and symbolic framing dictate public acceptance, regulatory exposure, and market viability. Unmanned aerial systems (UAS) represent a primary case study in this dynamic. As hard material artifacts, these flying machines are thoroughly cultural from their inception, serving as expressions of the very aspirations and anxieties they are meant to address. When state actors, defense contractors, commercial enterprises, and media outlets deploy specific nomenclatures, they engage in strategic framing designed to legitimize state action, sanitize physical risk, or unlock commercial capability. Understanding these discursive levers is an essential mandate for public affairs professionals managing complex, high-stakes technology portfolios.

#### Terminology Contestation: The Battle for Nomenclature

A fierce linguistic contest surrounds the naming of unmanned aircraft. Trade associations, military leaders, commercial startups, and journalists deploy divergent lexical strategies to shape public imagination and policy outcomes. Industry groups like the Association for Unmanned Vehicle Systems International (AUVSI) and commercial entrepreneurs have historically waged war against the word **"Drone."** For commercial stakeholders—such as Ray Mann, a Welsh engineer and owner of a civilian airport for drones, and Lucien Miller, CEO of Innov-8tive Designs in Vista, California—the term "drone" carries sinister military connotations linked to remote strikes and collateral damage. Miller expressed this commercial anxiety directly, contrasting civilian market ambitions against public perceptions of _"Predators and their Hellfire missiles bombing daycare centers in Afghanistan."_ Mann similarly warned against the label, arguing, _"Don't call them drones. I don't like the word. It makes people fearful. It has a sinister sound to it."_ To insulate commercial enterprises from military stigma, industry advocates championed technical euphemisms like **Unmanned Aerial System (UAS)** or **Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV)**. This institutional anxiety peaked at the 2013 AUVSI convention in Washington, D.C., where press room Wi-Fi credentials were set to the password `DONTSAYDRONE`.

Conversely, military leadership rejected "unmanned" framing for entirely different reasons. Senior defense officials, including U.S. Air Force General Norton Schwartz and U.S. Army General Martin Dempsey, explicitly barred the use of both "drone" and "unmanned aerial system." During a 2012 interview, General Schwartz corrected a reporter by stating, _"Drones mischaracterize what these things are. They're not dumb. Nor are they unmanned, actually. They're remotely piloted aircraft."_ General Dempsey reinforced this stance at NATO headquarters, asserting, _"You will never hear me use the word drone, and you'll never hear me use the term 'unmanned aerial systems,' because they are not. They are remotely piloted aircraft."_ Military institutions mandated the term **Remotely Piloted Aircraft (RPA)** to professionalize public perception, re-anchor human moral responsibility, and highlight the presence of highly trained pilots in control of precision assets.

Meanwhile, journalists and the general public consistently favored **"Drone"**, finding technical acronyms clinical or evasive. Technology reporter Joe Pappalardo illustrated this persistent public apprehension in _Popular Mechanics_, recounting a conversation with a New York City police officer and Afghan War veteran on a boat. When Pappalardo attempted to highlight peaceful civilian uses, the officer flatly rejected the reassurance, stating: _"If I saw one of these while I was out on my boat, I'd just, you know, shoot it down."_ This encounter highlights how deep-seated law enforcement and military associations are embedded in the popular lexicon, even as consumer media and foreign tabloids occasionally experimented with humanizing oxymorons like "cuddly flying spy bot" to disarm surveillance fears.

#### Theoretical Framework Integration

To dissect these linguistic struggles, critical discourse analysis relies on Norman Fairclough’s Tripartite CDA Framework, which evaluates communication across three distinct, overlapping dimensions:

1. **Text:** The formal micro-linguistic features, syntax, wording, and metaphors. This includes **nominalization**—such as the phrase "drone strike," which transforms an action into an abstract event, systematically erasing the active human agent responsible for launching the missile—and **collocation**, where habitual word pairings like "Islamic militant," "Muslim extremist," or "high-value target" establish ideological frameworks within news coverage.
2. **Discursive Practice:** The institutional processes of text production, distribution, and consumption. This encompasses _intertextuality_ (how news stories explicitly cite legal white papers or popular culture) and _interdiscursivity_ (the blending of national security briefs, corporate PR copy, and science-fiction tropes to make sense of new technologies).
3. **Social Practice:** The overarching socio-political and economic structures that shape and are shaped by discourse, such as the expansion of executive war powers, state secrecy regimes, and global defense market expansion.

Drone discourse is further driven by the concept of the **Technological Sublime** (Leo Marx, David Nye)—a preferred trope in American cultural history wherein technological achievements evoke a dual reaction of awe, wonder, and transcendent promise alongside profound terror, apprehension, and moral dread. The technological sublime represents a discursive construct that articulates a society's political and moral relation to innovation.

This cultural ambivalence is actively pre-framed through **Anticipatory Discourse**—narratives that prophesize, imagine, and lay claim to future technological realities:

- **Ancient Mythology:** Cultural antecedents span millennia. Greek mythology introduced terrifying winged predators doing the bidding of gods, such as _Harpies_. Islamic tradition in tribal regions invokes the _Ababeel_, mythic birds described in the Quran that defended Mecca by dropping stones on invading armies—a term contemporary Waziri tribesmen explicitly apply to overhead drones. Prehistoric weapons like Freyr's magical self-fighting sword and Thor's target-seeking hammer _Mjölnir_ in Norse mythology, alongside the ancient Sumerian _Sharur_ (a sentient, flying, information-gathering mace that communicated with its wielder), established early imaginative blueprints for semi-automated, long-range killing machines.
- **Nikola Tesla’s Telautomatics:** At the 1898 Electrical Exhibition at Madison Square Garden, Tesla unveiled his radio-controlled _teleautomaton_ boat. Writing in _Century Magazine_ in 1900 ("The Problem of Increasing Human Energy"), Tesla articulated the concept of **"telautomatics"**—embodying the operator's **"borrowed mind"** within a distant machine capable of acting with intelligence and judgment. Tesla envisioned a sublime future where automated machines would replace human combatants, naively predicting that machine-on-machine warfare would yield universal peace.
- **Science Fiction Antecedents:** Modern public reception is heavily pre-framed by literary sci-fi. George Orwell’s _1984_ established the trope of omnipresent, sky-borne state surveillance. Philip K. Dick’s _Vulcan's Hammer_ depicted swarming, unseen mechanical entities pursuing individuals like vengeful wasps, tapping into paranoid cultural anxieties. Ian Watson’s 1985 short story _Slow Birds_ accurately portended the physical composite skin, loitering capability, and psychological dread experienced by populations living under the persistent presence of overhead military drones.

By examining these theoretical foundations, public affairs specialists can see how ancient mythologies and early industrial spectacles pre-configured the modern imagination, setting the stage for the strategic communications strategies deployed during the War on Terror.

### 2. Military Framing and the Rhetoric of Perpetual War

#### Context & Strategic Mandate

State actors and defense institutions must continuously construct legitimating narratives for novel military technologies, particularly during fundamental shift points in warfare. Managing public consent is a strategic imperative when introducing remote-control combat platforms. By deploying specialized legal lexicons, technocratic euphemisms, and controlled media narratives, executive authorities legitimize extrajudicial operations, sanitize state violence, and minimize political friction. However, when strategic communicators over-promise technical perfection, they create institutional liabilities; operational failures or civilian casualty reports quickly shatter sanitized narratives, exposing the state to severe public and legislative backlash.

#### Policy Evolution & Political Legitimation: Bush vs. Obama Administrations

##### The Bush Era: Anticipatory Discourse and Military Transformation

The discursive groundwork for weaponized drones was established prior to their widespread combat operationalization. In September 1999, GOP presidential candidate George W. Bush delivered a defense address at The Citadel titled "A Period of Consequences." The speech operated as explicit anticipatory discourse. Bush called for a "revolution in the technology of war," promising to create a military that would "skip a generation" by investing in long-range, high-tech, precision-guided, and unmanned systems to "redefine war on our terms."

Following the attacks of September 11, 2001, Bush returned to The Citadel on December 11, 2001. The White House executed a strategic communications push, emailing journalists the text of the 1999 speech to frame the president as a prescient leader whose vision was now validated by early deployments of armed RQ-1 Predators in Afghanistan. Mainstream news outlets adopted this framing, celebrating high-tech assets that promised to revolutionize combat by combining real-time intelligence with instant strike capabilities.

##### The Obama Era: Technocratic Precision and Normalized Targeted Killing

Upon taking office in 2009, the Obama administration executed a linguistic rebranding effort, directing the Department of Defense to abandon "Global War on Terror" or "Long War" in favor of the technocratic label **"Overseas Contingency Operations."** Despite attempting to distance itself from Bush-era rhetoric, the Obama administration dramatically escalated drone operations, conducting over ten times the number of drone strikes as its predecessor. Journalists across major publications quickly dubbed the armed drone Barack Obama’s **"weapon of choice."**

To maintain public support, the Obama administration leveraged executive secrecy alongside strategic, selective leaks. CIA Director Leon Panetta normalized the program by famously characterizing drone strikes as _"the only game in town"_ for defeating al-Qaeda networks. Targeted killing was framed as technologically precise, efficient, moral, and "clean"—a stark contrast to ground invasions or the politically disastrous detention and "enhanced interrogation" policies of the Bush era. However, this reliance on technocratic language ("surgical precision", "kinetic operations") created major messaging vulnerabilities when independent civilian casualty reports emerged, exposing the gap between executive rhetoric and battlefield realities.

#### Critical Discourse Moments

##### 1. The 2002 Zhawar Kili Strike (Afghanistan)

- **Incident Deconstruction:** In February 2002, a CIA-operated Predator drone fired a Hellfire missile at a "tall man" surrounded by deferential followers in Zhawar Kili, Afghanistan, sparking media speculation that Osama bin Laden had been eliminated. Local reports immediately contradicted this narrative, revealing the dead were actually impoverished local peasants scavenging for scrap metal.
- **Discursive Management & Media Spin:** Pentagon officials, led by Rear Admiral John Stufflebeem, deployed deflection tactics. Rather than acknowledging intelligence failures, the Pentagon defended the strike as "appropriate," asserting that search teams recovered airline schedules, credit card applications, and small arms at the site, implying "something untoward" was underway. News coverage shifted public focus from civilian casualties toward high-stakes drama surrounding DNA testing of the victims, successfully diluting accountability for intelligence miscalculations.

##### 2. The 2002 Marib Province Strike (Yemen)

- **Incident Deconstruction:** On November 4, 2002, a CIA Predator drone destroyed a vehicle in Marib Province, Yemen, killing senior al-Qaeda operative Qaed Salim Sinan al-Harethi alongside five others. Among the dead was Kamal Derwish, a dual U.S.-Yemeni citizen linked by federal authorities to a "homegrown" terror cell based in Lackawanna / Buffalo, New York. This marked the first targeted drone strike outside an active war zone.
- **Linguistic Emergence:** Coverage of this strike catalyzed two major linguistic shifts in Western media:
  - **"Robotic Warfare":** Quoting defense experts like Clifford Beal of _Jane's Defence Weekly_ ("This is the beginning of robotic warfare"), headlines sensationalized the era of "quiet assassins" and autonomous executioners.
  - **"Targeted Killing":** Media outlets adopted the phrase "targeted killing"—a term borrowed directly from Israeli military strategies previously condemned by Washington. State Department spokesman David Boucher was forced into linguistic gymnastics to defend the U.S. strike while attempting to maintain official opposition to Israeli extrajudicial operations, asserting that U.S. justifications "do not necessarily apply in other circumstances."

##### 3. The 2011 Assassination of Anwar al-Awlaki and Samir Khan

- **Incident Deconstruction:** On September 30, 2011, a U.S. drone strike in Yemen killed radical Muslim cleric Anwar al-Awlaki and magazine editor Samir Khan—both American citizens who had never been formally charged with a crime or granted a trial.
- **Media Framing vs. Civil Liberties Dissent:** Mainstream U.S. news accounts largely adopted executive branch framing, celebrating the strike as a "major blow" to terrorism and echoing official quotes that _"a very bad man just had a very bad day."_ Media profiles heavily focused on al-Awlaki’s journey from "all-American boy" to the "YouTube Bin Laden." Conversely, civil rights groups (such as the ACLU and the Center for Constitutional Rights) issued sharp critiques, labeling the execution of U.S. citizens without judicial process an unconstitutional erosion of due process. However, mainstream media routinely marginalized these objections, placing civil liberties commentary at the end of reports while presenting executive strike authority as a necessary, clean alternative to traditional warfare.

#### Deep-Dive Analysis: The Elasticity of Legal Discourse

In February 2013, a leaked 16-page Department of Justice (DOJ) White Paper exposed the legal framework developed by the Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) to justify targeting U.S. citizens abroad. Critical discourse analysis reveals that the executive branch systematically manipulated core legal terms, creating an "elastic legal discourse" that effectively stripped standard words of their accepted meanings. From a public affairs standpoint, this linguistic manipulation represented a severe strategic risk; while it provided temporary administrative cover, it severely eroded institutional credibility and triggered significant legislative exposure:

- **"Imminent Threat":** Traditionally, "imminent" denotes an immediate, impending physical threat (hours or days). The DOJ white paper created a "broader concept of imminence," asserting that the U.S. government does not require clear evidence of an active, specific attack in progress to strike a target, expanding the concept into an open-ended temporal window.
- **"Infeasible":** Rather than denoting physical impossibility, "infeasible capture" was redefined to mean any operation that might present an "undue risk" to U.S. personnel or where a host nation failed to cooperate, rendering non-lethal apprehension operationally inconvenient.
- **"Due Process":** The white paper argued that executive-branch administrative deliberations satisfied Fifth Amendment constitutional due process requirements, entirely bypassing judicial review or oversight before or after a lethal strike.

#### Elite Disruption & Media Theater

This elastic legal reasoning generated a rare breakdown in elite political consensus in early 2013, triggering two major public affairs events:

1. **Senator Rand Paul’s Filibuster (March 2013):** Prompted by an ambiguous letter from Attorney General Eric Holder regarding domestic strike authority, Senator Rand Paul executed a 13-hour filibuster against CIA Director nominee John Brennan. Questioning whether the executive branch possessed constitutional authority to target Americans on domestic soil, Paul's speech (#StandWithRand) operated as disruptive political theater. Intertextually referencing _Mr. Smith Goes to Washington_, the filibuster temporarily united civil libertarians, conservative politicians, and independent media outlets in opposition to unchecked executive authority.
2. **Obama’s National Defense University (NDU) Address (May 2013):** To manage public fallout and re-establish elite consensus, President Obama delivered a major address at NDU outlining official Presidential Policy Guidelines (PPG). Obama acknowledged that perpetual war via drones would prove self-defeating and promised greater oversight, transparency, and an eventual repeal of the 2001 AUMF. The address successfully co-opted reform discourse, re-establishing administrative legitimacy even as core operational practices remained largely intact.

These foreign military messaging strategies provided the operational template when commercial stakeholders sought to integrate unmanned systems into domestic airspace.

### 3. The Discourse of Domestication: PR Strategies and Cultural Integration

#### Context & Strategic Mandate

When a novel technology emerges from a military context, commercial stakeholders face the imperative of **"domestication"**—the systematic process of stripping an artifact of its military stigma, neutralizing public apprehension, and reframing it as an essential, safe consumer product or industrial asset. Commercial drone integration required industry advocates to execute strategic communications campaigns designed to build market acceptance and secure a favorable regulatory environment from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). Public affairs practitioners facilitate this transition by linking the novel technology to familiar consumer platforms, directly framing regulatory hurdles around economic necessity and manageable risk.

#### Industry PR & Normalization Analogies

Trade associations like AUVSI led this domestication effort. AUVSI commissioned economic impact studies predicting that domestic drone integration would generate over **$82 billion** in economic activity and create **100,000 new jobs** by 2025. Industry advocates pivoted messaging away from defense applications toward high-utility, non-threatening sectors, specifically highlighting "precision agriculture" (crop monitoring, fertilizer application) and search-and-rescue operations.

To re-anchor public perception, industry leaders systematically deployed historical normalization analogies within a structured public relations narrative:

- **The Smartphone Analogy (Chris Anderson /** _**Wired**_**):** Anderson framed consumer quadcopters as "flying smartphones." By highlighting shared components—cheap microprocessors, GPS modules, accelerometers, and cameras derived from the trillion-dollar mobile phone supply chain—Anderson argued that drones were simply democratized, open-source consumer electronics that users could harness for creative and industrial productivity.
- **The Personal Computer Analogy (Michael Toscano / AUVSI):** Toscano framed privacy concerns and rogue operations as unavoidable side-effects of an indispensable technology. Just as personal computers introduced identity theft, malware, and illicit content without diminishing the technology's societal necessity, Toscano argued that society must hold "bad actors" accountable rather than restricting the underlying drone platform.
- **The Automobile Analogy (Michael Toscano / AUVSI):** Toscano deployed a risk-acceptance frame to normalize physical hazards. He noted that despite automobiles causing over 33,000 traffic fatalities annually in the United States, society willingly accepts this physical risk due to the immense economic and personal utility cars provide. Unmanned systems, he argued, require a similar framework of societal risk-acceptance.

#### Incident Management & Policy Outcomes

The domestic communications strategy faced a severe crisis on January 26, 2015, when an intoxicated off-duty employee of the **National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA)** accidentally crashed a recreational DJI Phantom quadcopter onto the White House lawn at 3 AM. The incident triggered facility-wide security lockdowns and national media headlines, highlighting the supreme irony of an intelligence agency employee crashing a commercial drone at the Executive Mansion.

Commercial advocates immediately launched an incident management pivot. Industry spokespeople framed the crash as a harmless mistake by a recreational user, leveraging the event to argue that the absence of clear operational guidelines created public confusion. President Obama echoed this messaging in a CNN interview, calling on the FAA to expedite regulatory frameworks to unlock commercial potential.

This public relations management culminated in June 2016 when the FAA released its landmark **Part 107 rules** for commercial drone operation. The regulation eliminated the requirement for commercial operators to hold a manned aircraft pilot’s license—a major historic barrier to entry—thereby opening the sky to realtors, farmers, filmmakers, and inspection firms. Meanwhile, high-concept commercial announcements—such as Jeff Bezos announcing Amazon Prime Air during a December 1, 2013 interview with Charlie Rose on CBS’s _60 Minutes_ right before "Cyber Monday"—further drove public expectations, despite lingering FAA prohibitions on fully autonomous delivery.

#### Popular Culture as Cultural Barometer

Entertainment media and commercial advertising serve as vital barometers of public perception, reflecting and shaping popular attitudes toward unmanned technology.

##### Corporate Advertising

- **Audi’s "The Drones" Commercial (2015):** Audi released a prime-time television commercial explicitly modeling Alfred Hitchcock’s 1963 film _The Birds_. In the ad, terrified office workers are swarmed and attacked outside their building by aggressive parcel-carrying delivery quadcopters. The protagonist escapes using the advanced technology of his Audi A6. The commercial’s tagline—_"Technology doesn't have to be intimidating"_—captured ambient cultural paranoia surrounding delivery initiatives while positioning the automobile as a familiar, protective technology.

##### Film & Television Representation

Popular entertainment continuously oscillates between reinforcing national security anxieties and humanizing remote operators:

- **Action Blockbusters (**_**Iron Man 2**_**,** _**Star Trek Into Darkness**_**,** _**Captain America: The Winter Soldier**_**):** These films dramatize high-level anxieties regarding autonomous systems and state overreach. _Iron Man 2_ features rogue "Hammer Drones" that turn on civilian crowds; _Star Trek Into Darkness_ allegorizes targeted killing ethics when Captain Kirk and Spock debate whether to execute fugitive John Harrison with long-range torpedoes on Qo'noS or capture him for trial; and _Captain America: The Winter Soldier_ directly critiques preemptive strike doctrines through algorithmic "Helicarriers." The debate is explicitly captured in dialogue between Captain America (_"I thought the punishment usually came after the crime"_) and Nick Fury (_"We're going to neutralize a lot of threats before they even happen"_).
- **Political Thrillers (**_**Homeland**_**,** _**24**_**):** _Homeland_ examines the psychological complexity of remote warfare, crowning protagonist Carrie Mathison "The Drone Queen." Crucially, civilian collateral damage—specifically the drone strike that killed Brody's young student, **Issa**—serves as the primary narrative catalyst for his radicalization.
- **Satirical Comedy (**_**Arrested Development**_**):** Season 4 lampoons operator dissociation by depicting Buster Bluth operating military drones under the belief that he is playing an arcade video game, satirizing the sanitized, detached nature of remote warfare.

These insights into cultural and commercial framing directly inform actionable guidance for strategic communicators managing high-stakes technological portfolios.

### 4. Strategic Takeaways and Communications Playbook

#### Context & Strategic Mandate

For strategic communications officers, public relations managers, and corporate affairs executives operating in high-stakes technology sectors, media discourse analysis yields actionable intelligence. Unmanned aerial systems demonstrate how quickly messaging vulnerabilities can paralyze an industry or invite heavy regulatory burdens. When organizations over-promise technological capabilities, rely on clinical euphemisms, or ignore underlying societal fears, they create severe brand and policy vulnerabilities.

1. **The Euphemism Trap:** Relying on clinical or institutional jargon (e.g., "RPA" or "UAS") to force public adoption while banning popular vocabulary (as seen in AUVSI's `DONTSAYDRONE` press room blunder) generates media mockery and projects institutional dishonesty.

4. **Ignoring Blowback and Anti-System Activism:** Failing to proactively address systemic risks (such as privacy intrusion, noise pollution, or remote operator psychological trauma) cedes the narrative high ground to investigative journalists, activists, and satirists.

#### Golden Rules for Communicating Dual-Use Technologies

#### 1. Respect the Power of Public Nomenclature

**Never wage war on the public lexicon.** Attempting to forcibly replace common vocabulary with clinical corporate euphemisms or military acronyms signals institutional guilt and invites media cynicism. Instead, adopt popular terms directly and consistently anchor them to positive, grounded, and human-centric applications.

#### 2. Ground Operational Claims in Transparent Governance

**Avoid the trap of pristine perfection.** Promising absolute precision, zero risk, or infallible automation guarantees catastrophic PR fallout when technical or human failures occur. Proactively acknowledge operational limits, define terms transparently without linguistic distortion, and emphasize rigorous human oversight.

#### 3. Neutralize Stigma Through Familiar Analogies

**Domesticate complex innovation by linking it to ubiquitous, accepted technologies.** Frame novel, intimidating platforms in the context of familiar historical consumer shifts—such as smartphones, personal computers, or automobiles. Explicitly demonstrate how societal benefits outweigh managed risks, and highlight technical safeguards that protect public safety and civil liberties.

#### 1.2 Theoretical Foundation: Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) and Social Constructionism

To fully evaluate how policy authority is constructed around aerial technology, one must apply the analytical tools of Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA). Language is fundamentally functional; choice of wording, syntactic structure, and narrative framing carry direct ideological consequences. In technology policy, discourse does not merely reflect preexisting material artifacts, but actively constitutes public knowledge and structures the boundaries of permissible state action.

This analysis relies upon Norman Fairclough's tripartite CDA framework, which integrates three distinct dimensions:

1. **The Text:** The formal linguistic features of communication. Crucial micro-linguistic mechanisms include **nominalization**—the process of transforming active verbs into nouns (e.g., converting the violent act of state killing into the abstract noun phrase _"drone strike"_), which systematically erases human agency and obscures state responsibility—and ideological **collocations**, such as routinely coupling _"Muslim"_ or _"tribal"_ with _"extremist"_, _"radical cleric"_, or _"militant"_ in news discourse to construct naturalized categories of legitimate targets.
2. **Discursive Practice:** The institutional mechanisms governing the production, distribution, and consumption of media texts. This incorporates concepts of intertextuality, interdiscursivity (blending news reporting with science fiction tropes), and intertextual chains (tracking how leaked memos or official briefings travel across media ecosystems).
3. **Social Practice:** The overarching political, economic, and institutional structures—the "social matrix of discourse"—that determine power relations, state sovereignty, and market expansion.

Fairclough's framework synthesizes James Carey's social constructionist paradigm, which posits that reality is brought into existence and maintained through symbolic communication. As Carey notes, technology is thoroughly cultural from its inception, acting as an expression of societal aspirations rather than a mere material instrument. Furthermore, Charlotte Epstein defines discourse as a cohesive ensemble of ideas, concepts, and categorizations about a specific object that frames that object in a definitive way, thereby delimiting the range of legal, political, and strategic possibilities for action.

Media discourse, therefore, does not passively mirror technological evolution. By constructing specific narratives of precision, necessity, or economic inevitability, public and institutional talk transforms remote aerial artifacts into potent symbols of state power, imperial authority, or corporate innovation. In doing so, it delimits policy debates, rendering alternative legal frameworks or radical anti-war critiques marginal or unthinkable. From this analytical foundation, the social construction of remote aerial technology moves directly into the realm of ideological perception through the psychological construct of the technological sublime.

#### 1.3 The Rhetoric of the Technological Sublime

The public imagination surrounding remote aerial technology is deeply structured by what cultural theorists Leo Marx and David Nye term the "technological sublime." Historically embedded in the American experience, the sublime describes a complex psychological and cultural response to monumental human achievements or natural sites—an experience characterized by simultaneous awe, wonder, and terror.

Leo Marx demonstrated how 19th-century industrialization replaced early agrarian pastoral ideals with an official ideology of industrialism, imbuing machines with moral and spiritual meaning. The machine was framed as a "virtuous machine," capable of guaranteeing national unity, moral health, and inevitable progress. David Nye expanded this analysis by categorizing various historical iterations of the sublime, notably the "dynamic sublime" (associated with the vast physical power and speed of the railroad) and the "electric sublime" (associated with the space-annihilating communication of the telegraph, wireless systems, and urban electrification).

Modern remote aerial technology represents a 21st-century synthesis of both the dynamic and electric sublimes. Drones combine the dynamic physical power of airborne weapon platforms with the electric sublime of instantaneous, global satellite communications and real-time high-definition sensor streams. Media narratives leverage this sublime framing to construct a compelling narrative of the "virtuous machine." This narrative promises:

- **Annihilation of Space and Time:** The capacity to project power, gather intelligence, and strike targets anywhere across the globe instantaneously without deploying ground troops.
- **Absolute Surgical Precision:** The illusion of clinical, error-free warfare that eliminates threat actors cleanly without collateral damage.
- **Risk-Free National Defense:** The elimination of physical risk for domestic military personnel, transforming combat into a safe, remote procedure.

By framing remote aerial technology through the lens of the technological sublime, mainstream discourse obscures the profound moral, legal, and human costs of automated surveillance and remote killing. This cultural fascination creates a psychological runway that prepares the public to accept technological developments long before operational deployment, relying heavily on anticipatory narratives.

Cultural narratives historically anticipate, legitimize, and shape emerging military technologies long before those technologies become material realities. The historical trajectory of remote aerial technology reveals a continuous lineage of cultural anticipations:

- **Ancient Mythology:** Mythological traditions across cultures featured fearsome, invisible, or remote flying weapons. Greek mythology depicted Harpies—predatory avian hybrids—while Islamic tradition recalled the _ababeel_, mythic birds that defended Mecca by dropping stones on invading forces. Weaponry, too, was endowed with remote intelligence: Norse myth featured Thor's _Mjölnir_ (a targeting hammer that always returned) and Freyr's Sword (which fought autonomously), while Sumerian mythology described the _Sharur_ ("smasher of thousands"), a sentient, flying mace that gathered intelligence and communicated with its wielder. Modern defense projects directly inherit these tropes, evidenced by DARPA mimicking goshawks and insects, or weapons systems named _Gorgon Stare_ and _Harpy_.
- **Nikola Tesla's Telautomatics and Philosophical Frame:** In 1898 at Madison Square Garden, Nikola Tesla demonstrated his radio-controlled "teleautomaton" boat. Writing in _Century Magazine_ in 1900 on "telautomatics," Tesla described machines embodying a **"borrowed mind"**—where the operator's judgment and intelligence were embodied in the distant machine. Tesla naively hypothesized that this technology would achieve **universal peace** by replacing human combatants with machines, asserting that future war would consist of machine fighting machine. Tesla's utopian assumption stands in stark contrast to today's material reality of a global "killerbot arms race" and the 2015 open letter signed by over 20,000 AI researchers and public intellectuals (including Stephen Hawking and Stuart Russell) warning of autonomous weapons systems.
- **Science Fiction & Military Planning:** Early science fiction pioneers Jules Verne and H.G. Wells established the long-arc evolutionary imaginary of airborne technological projection. 20th-century literature further pre-figured these systems: George Orwell's _1984_ imagined ubiquitous airborne police surveillance. Philip K. Dick's 1960 novel _Vulcan's Hammer_ depicted swarming, wasp-like mechanical entities flitting at the edges of vision to hunt individuals. Ian Watson's prescient 1985 short story _Slow Birds_ described Predator-like aircraft: silvery grey, tubular metal bodies with rounded heads, finned tails, and stubby wings that appeared unpredictably to patrol skies.

These cultural imaginaries provide the structural template for defense planning. Contemporary defense institutions actively blur the line between science fiction and material development. The Marine Corps Warfighting Lab derived wristwatch video feed technology directly from _Dick Tracy_ comic strips, while military institutions consult with science fiction writers through entities like SIGMA—a defense think tank composed of sci-fi authors (such as Greg Bear and Arlan Andrews) advising DARPA and the Department of Homeland Security on future threats. This anticipatory framework allowed military planners to present semi-autonomous warfare as a natural and inevitable evolution when the geopolitical landscape shifted post-9/11.

On September 23, 1999, GOP presidential candidate George W. Bush delivered a major defense address at The Citadel titled "A Period of Consequences." Designed to establish foreign policy credibility, the speech introduced key rhetorical tropes that would define 21st-century defense posture. Bush declared that the nation must "skip a generation" of conventional weaponry, pursue "military transformation," and harness a "revolution in the technology of war." He explicitly envisioned a military capable of striking "from across the world with pinpoint accuracy—with long-range aircraft and perhaps with unmanned systems." Neoconservative advisors, including Dick Cheney, Paul Wolfowitz, Donald Rumsfeld, and Condoleezza Rice, authored and backed this vision.

On December 11, 2001—the three-month anniversary of the 9/11 attacks—President Bush returned to The Citadel. In an orchestrated campaign of strategic communication, Press Secretary Ari Fleischer e-mailed reporters the text of the 1999 speech, constructing a deliberate **intertextual chain** to manufacture an aura of political "prescience." Commentators like George Stephanopoulos and Ron Brownstein largely adopted this frame, praising Bush's foresight. Bush framed the ongoing invasion of Afghanistan (Operation Enduring Freedom) as a "proving ground" for transformation, explicitly praising the armed RQ-1 Predator drone. Early operational successes, such as the November 2001 strike killing al-Qaeda leader Mohammed Atef, were seized upon by media outlets, which described the aircraft as an "ugly duckling" transformed into a formidable weapon, setting the operational precedent for targeted strikes.

#### 2.3 Critical Discourse Moments: Early Targeted Strikes and the Adoption of Israeli Tactics

As the Bush administration expanded remote operations, early tactical strikes established legal precedents and press coverage patterns that fundamentally altered international norms. Two specific critical discourse moments highlight the mechanics of military spin and legal adaptation:

##### The February 2002 Zhawar Kili Strike (Afghanistan)

A CIA-operated Predator drone targeted and killed three men in the Zhawar Kili region of eastern Afghanistan after observing a "tall man" treated with deference, suspected of being Osama bin Laden. Subsequent investigations revealed the victims were poor Afghan civilians scavenging for scrap metal.

Military press operations immediately launched damage-control spin. Rear Admiral John Stufflebeem defended the attack as "appropriate," deflecting intelligence failure by claiming search teams recovered credit card applications and airline schedules, suggesting "something untoward" was underway. News outlets focused heavily on speculative efforts to obtain bin Laden family DNA rather than interrogating the failure of drone sensor identification.

##### The November 2002 Yemen Strike

A CIA Predator executed a targeted strike against a jeep in Marib Province, Yemen, killing senior al-Qaeda operative Qaed Salim Sinan al-Harethi alongside five others, including Kamal Derwish, a dual Yemeni-American citizen associated with a Buffalo, New York terror cell.

Following the Yemen strike, U.S. policy explicitly adopted the Israeli framework. State Department spokesman David Boucher attempted to explain away the double standard by claiming that U.S. opposition to Israeli strikes rested on circumstances that did not apply in other contexts. Former CIA counsel Jeffrey Smith further normalized the practice by arguing on national public radio that ethics aside, high-level administrative approval ensured operational validity, substituting executive discretion for judicial process. Official rhetoric successfully shifted public debate away from human rights violations, establishing executive authority for covert executions that would expand under the subsequent administration.

### 3. Institutionalization, Secrecy, and Executive Authority Under the Obama Administration

_Turns out I'm really good at killing people._ — **Barack Obama**, discussing the drone program with aides, September 30, 2011

#### 3.1 The "Weapon of Choice" Paradigm and Rebranding the War on Terror

The presidency of Barack Obama presented a striking socio-political paradox: a constitutional law scholar and anti-war candidate who exponentially expanded covert aerial assassination. Upon taking office in March 2009, the Obama administration attempted a discursive rebranding of the conflict, issuing a Department of Defense memorandum directing staff to cease using the phrase "Global War on Terror" or "Long War," substituting the technocratic phrase "Overseas Contingency Operations."


Despite this linguistic shift toward administrative restraint, operational realities escalated dramatically. Between 2009 and 2015, President Obama authorized over 500 covert drone strikes—more than ten times the total conducted under the Bush administration. The strike campaign expanded aggressively beyond declared battlefields in Iraq and Afghanistan into undeclared conflict zones in Pakistan, Yemen, and Somalia.

This expansion was operationalized through institutional selection mechanisms revealed by the _New York Times_ (Becker & Shane, 2012): weekly **"Terror Tuesday"** meetings in the White House Situation Room, where President Obama personally vetted names on a secret kill list, and the creation of the **"Disposition Matrix"**—a database cross-referencing intelligence inputs to determine whether targets should be captured, interrogated, or extrajudicially executed by drone. Media coverage rapidly institutionalized this practice, uniformly characterizing the weaponized drone as Obama's "weapon of choice." CIA Director Leon Panetta publicly entrenched this paradigm by declaring targeted drone strikes "the only game in town" for disrupting al-Qaeda. By framing lethal remote attacks as the sole viable mechanism for counterterrorism, executive rhetoric naturalized targeted killing, shutting down non-lethal or diplomatic alternatives.

#### 3.2 Legal Rhetoric and the "Elastic" Language of Targeted Killing

To insulate the exponential expansion of targeted strikes from legal challenge—particularly regarding the execution of U.S. citizens—the Department of Justice (DOJ) Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) developed an "elastic" legal vocabulary. This framework was highlighted by the September 30, 2011 assassination of American-born cleric Anwar al-Awlaki and _Inspire_ editor Samir Khan in Yemen, followed weeks later in October 2011 by the targeted drone strike that killed al-Awlaki's 16-year-old American son, **Abdulrahman al-Awlaki**, while eating dinner at an outdoor restaurant. This legal architecture was formalized in a leaked 16-page DOJ White Paper obtained by NBC News reporter Michael Isikoff in February 2013.

The leak created a powerful **intertextual chain**, traveling from NBC News to civil rights organizations and onto the floor of the U.S. Senate. The OLC crafted highly elastic, distorted interpretations of core legal concepts to bypass statutory due process and constitutional protections:

#### Analysis of "Elastic" Legal Concepts in Executive Targeted Killing

- **"Imminent Threat":**
  - _OLC Redefinition:_ The Justice Department explicitly eliminated the traditional legal requirement of specific, immediate intelligence regarding an active, ongoing plot against the United States. Instead, "imminence" was stretched into a broad concept applying to generalized future potential, asserting that an operational leader poses a perpetual "imminent" threat simply by virtue of involvement with an associated force.
  - _Analytical Commentary:_ Legal scholar Mary Ellen O'Connell underscored that executing targets in non-battlefield situations violates fundamental international law, as imminence cannot be declared unilaterally without an active battlefield. As ACLU Legal Director Jameel Jaffer observed, this elastic definition grants the executive branch authority to kill citizens with "no geographic limit," converting a restrictive legal threshold into a perpetual license for preemptive extrajudicial execution anywhere on Earth.
- **"Infeasible" Capture:**
  - _OLC Redefinition:_ Capture was deemed "infeasible" not merely when physically impossible, but whenever operational personnel determined that arrest would involve "undue risk" to U.S. forces, political inconvenience, or host nation reluctance to cooperate.
  - _Analytical Commentary:_ By expanding "infeasibility" to incorporate political convenience and risk avoidance, the executive branch rendered lethal drone strikes the default operational choice rather than a true measure of last resort, replacing judicial apprehension with remote assassination.
- **"High-Level Official":**
  - _OLC Redefinition:_ The authority to order the execution of a U.S. citizen was assigned to an undisclosed, obscure "informed, high-level official" without specifying required rank, judicial qualification, or institutional position.
  - _Analytical Commentary:_ Leaving this designation obscure granted unprecedented latitude to executive agents, completely removing judicial review before or after the strike, and concentrating unilateral power over life and death within the executive branch.

Concurrently, a powerful counter-discourse of **witnessing** emerged to challenge official secrecy. In April 2013, Yemeni journalist and activist **Farea al-Muslimi** testified before a U.S. Senate subcommittee, describing how a drone strike on his village of Wessam terrorized innocent civilians and turned population sentiment against the United States. Furthermore, military drone whistleblowers brought to light the acute psychological trauma of operating optical feeds. Operators described a condition of **"distributed intimacy"**—where pilots spend weeks watching targets' daily family routines through high-resolution sensors before pulling the trigger, only to watch the graphic aftermath of video feed destruction (_"bug splat"_). This distributed intimacy produced severe moral injury and PTSD among operators, shattering the myth of risk-free, gamified warfare.

In response to this growing outcry, President Obama delivered a major address at National Defense University (NDU) on May 23, 2013, releasing the Presidential Policy Guidelines (PPG). Obama admitted that perpetual war through drones would prove self-defeating. However, as civil rights observers noted, the speech functioned primarily as calculated perception management, soothing public anxiety while preserving systemic operational leeway to continue remote aerial strikes globally.

### 4. The Discourse of Domestication: Industry Strategy and Commercial Integration

#### 4.1 Destigmatizing Drones: Economic Forecasting and Technocratic Analogies

As defense contractors and commercial entrepreneurs sought to expand remote aerial technology into domestic airspace, they faced a public apprehension fueled by news of overseas military assassinations. By reframing drones as benign economic tools that perform "dull, dirty, or dangerous" work, commercial actors sought to neutralize popular fear, shifting focus directly to economic potential.

Simultaneously, these security fears birthed a lucrative "counter-drone" industry. Companies offered geofencing platforms like NoFlyZone, while venture-capital-backed defense firms like Dedrone raised over $15 million to field systems that detect and disable unauthorized aircraft, proving that commercial integration inherently reproduces surveillance anxieties.

#### 5.2 Actionable Framework for Transparency, Nomenclature, and Oversight

Based on the critical discourse analysis of executive authority, institutional language, and domestic integration, the following actionable policy framework is recommended to realign remote aerial technology governance with constitutional process, legal integrity, and democratic accountability:

1. **Standardization of Legal Definitions and Elimination of Elastic Lexicons**
   - _Policy Directive:_ The Executive Branch and Department of Justice must formally abandon "elastic" interpretations of core legal terms crafted by the Office of Legal Counsel. The definition of "imminent threat" must return to its recognized international legal standard—requiring specific, credible, and immediate intelligence of an active, ongoing plot.
   - _Implementation:_ Capture infeasibility must not be invoked based on political inconvenience, host nation reluctance, or risk avoidance. Executive targeting decisions involving U.S. citizens or operations outside declared war zones must be subject to statutory due process standards and independent judicial review prior to authorization.
2. **Establishment of Mandated Transparency in Targeting Metrics and Casualty Reporting**
   - _Policy Directive:_ Establish an independent, statutory oversight body—such as a specialized judicial panel or Congressional reporting commission—with full authority to review CIA and military targeting logs, intelligence inputs, and casualty metrics.
   - _Implementation:_ Require the executive branch to publish mandatory quarterly reports detailing combatant versus non-combatant civilian casualties resulting from aerial strikes, removing absolute operational secrecy and facilitating genuine public and legal accountability.
3. **Regulatory Balance for Domestic Airspace and Surveillance Safeguards**
   - _Policy Directive:_ The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), in coordination with state legislative bodies, must construct commercial airspace regulations that rigorously protect public safety and citizen privacy rights without importing national security apparatus mechanisms.
   - _Implementation:_ Mandate strict statutory geofencing standards and privacy protections against state and corporate aerial surveillance. Commercial integration must be governed by statutory privacy frameworks that restrict persistent warrantless surveillance by law enforcement agencies.
4. **Institutional Lexical Integrity in Public Briefings**
   - _Policy Directive:_ Federal defense, intelligence, and law enforcement agencies must discontinue strategic perception management through sanitizing euphemisms in public communications.
   - _Implementation:_ Direct military and executive spokespersons to use precise, objective legal language in official briefings. Public briefings must clearly articulate operational agencies, targeting justifications, and specific weapon impacts, preventing the obfuscation of state actions behind technocratic terminology.

### Conclusion

The expansion of remote aerial technology represents a profound transformation in modern state power, legal normativity, and cultural visibility. Language is not a passive mirror of technological evolution; it is a primary instrument through which state and corporate actors manufacture authority, legitimize military escalation, and obscure human costs.

Deconstructing the linguistic maneuvers, historical imaginaries, and political rhetoric surrounding remote platforms reveals that safeguarding democratic authority requires continuous, rigorous interrogation of official language. Only through critical discourse analysis and robust, transparent oversight frameworks can public policy restrain executive overreach, protect constitutional rights, and ensure that technological innovation serves democratic society rather than unchecked state power.
