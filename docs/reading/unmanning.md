# **UNMANNING How Humans, Machines and Media Perform Drone Warfare** (Katherine Chandler) {#title}

[[toc]]

## Overview

![cover](https://i.imgur.com/DTTrrQO.png)

Katherine Chandler’s historical study examines how the modern **drone warfare system** was forged through early twentieth-century technological experiments and military planning. The text demonstrates that unmanned aerial vehicles are not simply objective machines, but rather **complex sociotechnical networks** integrating human operators, mechanical hardware, and media technologies to perform a specific script of war. Furthermore, the author analyzes how these projects relied on **disavowals of political reality and human action**, often masking strategic failures and high civilian casualties behind the illusion of clinical precision. Ultimately, the book provides a genealogy of **unmanned aerial vehicles** to reveal how historical experiments in remote-controlled flight and aerial surveillance continue to shape contemporary targeted killings and global military interventions.

## **Deconstructing the Drone: What "Unmanning" Really Means in Human-Machine Systems** {#what-is-unmanning}

### 1. Introduction: The Myth of the Autonomous Machine

Popular culture frequently depicts military drones as fully autonomous, self-thinking robots soaring through the sky completely independent of human intervention. Sci-fi tropes and defense industry marketing present a vision of machines that detect, track, and strike targets through algorithmic precision alone. However, the history of technology reveals a starkly different reality: drones are not self-contained, isolated artificial intelligence units, but distributed, highly fragile human-machine networks. To understand how these pilotless platforms truly function, media scholar and technology historian Katherine Chandler introduced the foundational theoretical concept of **"unmanning."**

**Unmanning:** A system design and political framing that conceals, hides, or disavows the ongoing human labor, operational support, and political decision-making required to make pilotless flight possible, rather than the actual removal of human effort from the loop.

In critical technology studies, **disavowal** refers to a design strategy and political maneuver where a state or institution acknowledges a technological operation while actively denying or minimizing the ongoing human agency, labor, and ethical responsibility behind it. Removing a human pilot from a physical cockpit does not eliminate human involvement; it merely relocates human operators to different nodes within a wider technological apparatus—shifting labor to ground control stations, field maintenance crews, radio relay stations, and remote intelligence analysts.

Furthermore, "unmanning" serves a profound political function. By framing an aircraft as a self-contained, automated machine, state institutions can obscure the human chain of command that authorizes military actions. As demonstrated when American U-2 pilot Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union in 1960, captured pilots present catastrophic political, diplomatic, and foreign policy fallout. Decades of military development—summarized in pivotal Cold War policy documents like the 1961 _Alternative Reconnaissance Systems_ report—explicitly sought pilotless platforms primarily to minimize political risk and public scrutiny, hiding state violence behind an illusion of automated objectivity.

This illusion of machine autonomy hides the reality that every pilotless aircraft relies on a distributed infrastructure. To understand how this hidden architecture operates in practice, we must first examine the three structural elements that make every drone function.

### 2. The Unmanning Triad: Humans, Machines, and Media

A drone cannot function as a standalone aircraft. Every pilotless system relies on an interconnected, three-part structural network known as the **Unmanning Triad**:

| System Element                         | Historical Role in Early Flight                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Why It Proves Humans Are Still Needed                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mechanical Aircraft (Machine)**      | The physical airframe, engine, onboard gyroscopes, and hydraulic **actuators** (mechanical devices that physically move flight surfaces like ailerons, rudders, and elevators).                                                                                                                    | Requires ground crews, mechanics, and structural engineers to construct, maintain, refuel, and physically repair mechanical components; onboard stabilization mechanisms frequently fail without human oversight.                                     |
| **Radio/Communications Media (Media)** | The connective infrastructure bridging physical distance, including radio frequencies, television camera feeds, directional antennas, and telephone-dial command boxes operating hydraulic **servo-valves** (electromechanical valves converting electrical signals into physical fluid movement). | Transmission media are inherently vulnerable to atmospheric noise, signal latency, line-of-sight limitations, and jamming; media links require continuous human management to transmit, interpret, and re-tune control signals across vast distances. |
| **Human Operators (Human)**            | Networks of ground pilots, radio technicians, visual spotters, intelligence analysts, and chase-plane controllers directing the aircraft's path.                                                                                                                                                   | Direct human judgment, sensory interpretation, manual command inputs, and strategic decisions drive the mission; when communications links break, the system collapses unless human operators intervene or establish recovery procedures.             |

This triad highlights the fundamental principle of media mediation: distance is never eliminated; it is merely reconfigured. Modern remote operators struggling with lag, transmission latency, and restricted "soda-straw" fields of view face direct modern equivalents of the technical friction that defined early pilotless flight. To see how these three components interacted during the foundational era of remote flight, we can turn to a pivotal case study from the 1930s.

### 3. Case Study: The 1930s "Nolo" Flights and the Hidden Human Network

In 1936, the U.S. Navy launched a classified research initiative designated "Project Drone," led by Officer-in-Charge Delmar Fahrney at the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL). Seeking a realistic, moving target to test shipboard anti-aircraft gunnery, Fahrney’s team set out to convert training biplanes into radio-controlled target aircraft. To describe these pilotless runs, Fahrney coined the term **"Nolo"**—a linguistic wordplay on "solo" standing for **"No Live Operator."**

During early experimental phases _prior_ to true pilotless flights, engineers utilized human "safety pilots" aboard modified NT-1 biplanes to test the radio equipment in mid-air. These early trials exposed extreme technical instability. In one infamous test, crossed aileron control wires caused the biplane to execute violent, erratic maneuvers that officers described as flying like a "drunken pilot," forcing the safety pilot to manually override the malfunctioning radio system to prevent a catastrophic crash.

Once these preliminary human-piloted trials were complete, the Navy transitioned to true "Nolo" flights—operating aircraft with completely empty cockpits. Deconstructing the operational chain of a typical 1930s Nolo flight exposes the massive human and mechanical infrastructure required to support a single "unmanned" target plane:

1. **Step 1: Takeoff & Ground Control** A ground-based radio pilot managed takeoff using a converted telephone-dial control box mounted on a field station truck (often a modified Chevrolet station wagon). The ground pilot dialed specific numbers to send pulsed radio signals ("beep control") to onboard hydraulic servo-valves, manually managing the engine throttle and elevator trim from the runway edge.
2. **Step 2: In-Flight Hand-off** Because the ground station was limited by visual line-of-sight, once the drone reached altitude, operational control was transferred via radio frequency to a secondary human controller aboard a tailing TG-2 control aircraft. Flying approximately one mile behind the drone, this airborne operator steered the target plane by keeping continuous visual contact through the cockpit windshield.
3. **Step 3: Redundant Safety Failsafes** To prevent the pilotless aircraft from flying out of control or crashing upon signal loss during true Nolo flights, engineers installed specialized hardware in the empty pilot's seat. This included a secondary 14-volt aircraft battery, duplicate radio receivers, and time-delay relays programmed to automatically put the plane into a banked turn if radio signals dropped for more than two minutes.
4. **Step 4: Landing & Frequent Crashes** Landing a pilotless aircraft proved immensely difficult because remote operators lacked direct physical feedback—such as engine vibration, wind resistance, and spatial depth perception. When ground pilots cut the throttle on approach, the drone's nose frequently dropped abruptly, shearing off the front wheel assembly and causing the aircraft to nose-over and slide violently across the landing strip.

There is deep historical irony in labeling these missions "No Live Operator." A flight designated as "Nolo" actually depended on dozens of field station operators, chase-plane pilots, radio technicians, maintenance mechanics, and gunnery crews to keep a single aircraft operational. Rather than eliminating human labor, "unmanning" merely dispersed human effort across a wide physical and technological network.

As World War II and the Cold War unfolded, military commanders and defense contractors attempted to expand this illusion, transforming pilotless platforms from simple target drones into weaponized assault systems and international surveillance platforms.

These historical failure modes directly parallel contemporary challenges in remote warfare. Just as 1944 television operators struggled with low-resolution monitors and 1950s controllers suffered from a lack of environmental feedback, modern remote pilots face high cognitive fatigue, spatial disorientation, and signal latency when attempting to navigate complex combat environments through limited monitor screens.

From the 1930s biplane to Cold War jet drones, the pattern remained unchanged: marketing language promised autonomous machine perfection, while operational reality required extensive human labor to manage system failures, poor communication links, and visual noise.

### 5. Conclusion: Key Conceptual Takeaways for Aspiring Learners

Deconstructing the history of "unmanning" provides vital analytical tools for evaluating modern remote warfare, robotics, and artificial intelligence. By looking past defense industry marketing and analyzing actual historical operations, technology students should master three primary conceptual takeaways:

1. **Drones Are Networks, Not Single Objects:** A drone is never just an isolated aircraft soaring through the sky. It is an interconnected system combining mechanical airframes, transmission media (radio waves, satellite links, screen feeds, and control signals), and distributed human labor. If any single node in this network fails or disconnects, the entire platform collapses.
2. **"Unmanned" is a Political Framing, Not a Technical Fact:** Removing a pilot from a physical cockpit is an engineering choice that shifts human effort to ground control stations, repair hangars, and intelligence centers. Labeling a system "unmanned" is a deliberate framing designed to conceal this ongoing human labor and disavow state accountability, portraying political decisions and military violence as objective, risk-free technical processes.
3. **Technology Does Not Eliminate Political or Operational Failure:** Re-labeling weapons or surveillance platforms as "automated," "electronic," or "pilotless" has historically been used to minimize foreign policy fallout and evade diplomatic scrutiny. Yet, remote technology does not neutralize human error, signal interference, visual misinterpretation, mechanical crashes, or ethical responsibility. Human agency remains present at every stage of design, operation, and command.

## **Deconstructing the Video-Targeting Paradox: An Executive Case Study of the February 2002 Predator Strike in Afghanistan** {#case-study}

### 1. Executive Context & Incident Overview

The strategic evolution of remote targeted killing represents a fundamental watershed in modern national security architecture, operating at the critical nexus of weaponized surveillance video, operator interpretation, and institutional authority. The kinetic engagement conducted by an armed MQ-1 Predator unmanned aerial vehicle on February 4, 2002, in the mountains of southeastern Afghanistan serves as a seminal case study in operational misidentification and cognitive closure. In this incident, the unrefined full-motion video feed of an uncrewed platform was immediately filtered through institutional expectations, converting raw visual data into an authoritative, high-confidence identification of high-value political enemies. The collision between remote visual streams and ground-level forensic reality demonstrates how real-time feeds generate an illusion of objective epistemic certainty—a digital panopticon that actively disarms critical inquiry and obscures fallible human interpretation, directly precipitating catastrophic operational errors.

| Official Pentagon / U.S. Government Claims                                                                                                                                           | Ground-Level Journalistic & Local Findings                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Target Designation:** Designated as Osama bin Laden or top-tier Al Qaeda leadership based on remote identification of a "tall, white-robed man" surrounded by a respectful detail. | **Victim Identification:** Positively identified by local communities as Daraz Khan (age ~31, known locally as "Tall Man"), Jehangir Khan (age ~28), and Mir Ahmed (age ~30).                |
| **Status Assertion:** Official military declarations from the Joint Chiefs of Staff and Pentagon press corps asserting that those killed were "not innocents."                       | **Militant Affiliation:** Impoverished local Afghan civilians with zero history, connection, or interest in militant Islamic politics or transnational terrorism.                            |
| **Operational Legitimacy:** Target appropriateness established through integrated real-time surveillance video, remote observation, and closed-loop intelligence feedback.           | **Ground Activity:** Economic survival activity; collecting abandoned scrap metal from previously bombed guerrilla cave sites to sell across the Pakistani border for 40 to 50 cents a load. |

The analytical evaluation ("So What?") of this strike highlights a profound systemic vulnerability in defense intelligence architectures. Immediately following the kinetic launch, early national media broadcasts and defense leadership linked the platform's video stream to high-value political targets—specifically speculating the elimination of Osama bin Laden. This cognitive framing created an overwhelming institutional bias toward target validation prior to physical, ground-level forensic verification. Operating under a presumption of technological infallibility, defense officials leveraged the platform’s real-time network integration to legitimize the strike, granting machinic authority to remote video feeds while systematically discounting the socio-economic and human context of the physical target environment.

This institutional bias established a precedent of bureaucratic insulation, bridging the general dynamics of remote target identification with the specific mechanical, organizational, and technocultural evolution of the weaponized Predator platform.

### 2. Operational Genesis & The February 2002 Strike Anatomy

Between 2000 and 2002, the Predator platform underwent a rapid institutional transition from an unarmed tactical reconnaissance airframe into an integrated "hunter-killer" weapon system. Originally engineered to gather imagery intelligence without risking human flight crews, the platform was re-engineered to compress the sensor-to-shooter loop—effectively eliminating the temporal latency between target detection and kinetic action. This technological synthesis transformed an intelligence-gathering platform into an instrument of standoff targeted killing, establishing an operational architecture where live video feeds were directly coupled with precision-guided munitions to authorize immediate strikes based on remote visual interpretations.

The genesis of this weaponized operational model traces to the summer of 2000, when an unarmed Predator captured hours of surveillance video over the Tarnak Farms complex outside Kandahar, Afghanistan. Defense analysts reviewing the live feed identified a "tall, white-robed man surrounded by a security detail," presumed to be Osama bin Laden. The video circulated through the highest echelons of military leadership—including the Secretary of the Air Force, the Chief of Staff, and the Assistant Vice Chief of Staff—catalyzing a bureaucratic effort to weaponize the platform. By February 2001, the Air Force executed live-fire integration testing at Creech Air Force Base in Indian Springs, Nevada, successfully marrying the Predator airframe, real-time video media, and laser-guided AGM-114 Hellfire missiles. The strategic shock of September 11, 2001, served as the ultimate catalyst to operationally deploy this integrated hunter-killer network over Afghan airspace.

A forensic ground investigation conducted by John F. Burns of the _New York Times_, alongside field testimonies from local Afghan eyewitnesses, yielded a rigorous forensic profile of the victims that utterly demolished the remote visual interpretation:

- **Daraz Khan (Age ~31):** Resident of Lalazha village. Known universally across the valley as "Tall Man"—a purely physical characteristic that directly triggered the remote operators' visual correlation with Osama bin Laden's recorded height profile.
- **Jehangir Khan (Age ~28):** Resident of Patalan village. Impoverished local civilian farmer and laborer with no militant ties or ideological affiliations.
- **Mir Ahmed (Age ~30):** Resident of Patalan village. Local civilian worker engaged in basic subsistence labor to support his family.
- **Socio-Economic Reality:** The three men were working as brushwood collectors and farmers gathering abandoned scrap metal from a former guerrilla cave complex that U.S. naval aircraft had bombed a month earlier. Villagers collected these twisted metal remnants to haul across the Pakistani border, earning a meager forty to fifty cents per load.
- **Qualitative Local Rejection:** Every Afghan encountered throughout the mountain valley—including goatherders, brushwood collectors, militiamen, and farmers—unhesitatingly identified the victims and their home villages. The U.S. government's high-value Al Qaeda claims were dismissed by the dead men's families, neighbors, and local anti-Taliban militiamen with explicit **"bitter mockery."**

Through the remote optics of the drone's sensory suite, the routine economic survival activity of poor villagers gathering scrap metal at a previously targeted military site—combined with Daraz Khan's physical height—was converted into visual threat indicators by operators thousands of miles away. Distant analysts translated non-hostile economic movement into tactical hostile intent, demonstrating how physical attributes are weaponized when filtered through low-resolution, screen-based media streams.

This fatal translation of ground reality into visual indicators of threat illustrates the exact bureaucratic mechanisms deployed by military institutions to defend the legitimacy of remote strikes when physical evidence repudiates initial targeting assumptions.

### 3. Institutional Legitimacy & Bureaucratic Justification Mechanisms

Military command structures utilize institutional discourse and technocratic authority to construct procedural "comfort zones" that shield operational legitimacy from forensic repudiation. By framing targeting decisions as the objective output of real-time intelligence feeds, leadership constructs a closed narrative loop. As philosopher Grégoire Chamayou observes, the drone apparatus "is nothing less than an understanding of politics," yet the technocratic performance of unmanning attempts to evacuate politics entirely, presenting fallible human decisions as the automated, neutral outputs of a technological network.

In congressional testimony supporting the fiscal year 2003 Department of Defense budget, Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld deployed this technocratic logic through his concept of **"Different Lethality."** Rumsfeld argued that replacing passive ISR platforms with armed variants capable of immediate kinetic execution established a seamless operational loop:

> _"If you have an unarmed Predator that's out there gathering intelligence information and you replace it with an armed Predator, that not only can gather intelligence information, but then can actually fire a Hellfire... you've got different lethality."_

Rumsfeld leveraged this concept internally within the Pentagon to secure expanded budget allocations and maximize operational latitude, selling the integration of surveillance and strike capabilities as a technological optimization. This rhetoric presented kinetic decision-making as an unassailable network function before the empirical strike accuracy of the system had been forensically established.

When journalistic investigations exposed the civilian identity of the February 4 victims, defense officials mobilized to preserve institutional legitimacy. On February 12, 2002, Rear Adm. John D. Stufflebeem (Deputy Director of Operations for the Joint Chiefs of Staff) publicly asserted that those killed were "not innocents." Stufflebeem explicitly detailed the institutional foundation of his confidence:

_"I base that on the facts that [the 101st Airborne]... did some exploration in the surrounding area, to include some caves, a nearby village, and talking to locals. So I think that that sort of puts us in a comfort zone. These were not innocents."_

Here, a brief post-strike ground sweep by the 101st Airborne Division was weaponized as an institutional shield—converting a cursory military sweep into an authoritative "comfort zone" that validated kinetic action. Simultaneously, Pentagon Press Secretary Victoria Clarke insisted: "We're convinced it was an appropriate target, based on the observation, based on the information that it was an appropriate target."

To sustain this institutional shielding, Pentagon officials systematically discounted testimonies from Afghan eyewitnesses, regional militiamen, and surviving family members. Pentagon spokespersons actively dismissed native intelligence by alleging that local Afghans routinely fabricated civilian casualty accounts to extract monetary compensation from the U.S. government. This systemic discounting maps directly onto historical patterns of imperial intervention and colonial power dynamics, wherein remote technological data harvested by state platforms is privileged over the lived, physical reality of native populations.

These institutional justification mechanisms rely directly on a broader technocultural framework—Katherine Chandler's theoretical model of "unmanning"—which abstracts human agency and political accountability through the visual media of the drone platform.

### 4. Technocultural Mechanics of "Unmanning" & The Visual Field

The theoretical concept of "unmanning"—formulated by national security scholar Katherine Chandler—does not signify the simple mechanical replacement or physical absence of a human pilot from an airframe. Rather, "unmanning" functions as a performative, triadic network operating continuously between human actors, machinic platforms, and media streams. This performance creates a powerful illusion of autonomous, objective technology while actively masking and disavowing the subjective human choices, cultural biases, and cognitive errors that govern operational deployment.

The constituent components of this triadic network operate as follows:

- **1. Human Component:** The remote operators, sensor operators, intelligence analysts, and command personnel who provide the interpretive context, visual assumptions, cognitive filters, and kinetic authorization.
- **2. Machine Component:** The physical Predator airframe, engine systems, onboard gyroscopes, laser target designators, and AGM-114 Hellfire missile hardware.
- **3. Media Component:** The optical and infrared video camera arrays, satellite transmission relays, frame-rate compression algorithms, and crosshair monitor interfaces displayed in the Ground Control Station (GCS).

The operational fiction of "unmanning" paradoxically collapses these three distinct vectors into an idealized, singular unit of objective feedback. In doing so, it grants absolute epistemic authority to screen-based visual outputs while disavowing the fallible human interpretations that drive the strike chain.

This screen-based visuality active-disarms critical inquiry through frame-rate compression, optical distance, and digital distortion. As geographer Derek Gregory famously deconstructs in his analysis of remote warfare, _"spaces of constructed visibility are also always spaces of constructed invisibility—because they are not technical but rather techno-cultural accomplishments."_ The drone's visual feed does not present a neutral optics window; it constructs a highly conditional, fallible view of the operational space. Screen optics render physical height and geographical proximity instantly visible while rendering the socio-economic, political, and human realities below—such as impoverished civilians collecting scrap metal to survive—entirely invisible.

This dynamic was captured by local Afghan militia commander Amir Khan in his critique of the February 2002 strike:

> _"This is theater, what the Americans have done here, just theater."_

Commander Khan’s critique diagnoses the performative dimension of targeted killing. The live video feed executes a spectacle of total battlefield mastery, establishing a regime of truth where screen-based visibility overrides ground-level physical facts.

This theoretical breakdown of visual interpretation errors in early 2002 directly shaped the operational practices, classification protocols, and legal precedents established in subsequent targeted killing operations across non-declared theaters of war.

### 5. Historical Precedent, Policy Failure, & Strategic Lessons

The severe operational errors and subsequent media exposures of February 2002 catalyzed a structural shift in U.S. targeted killing policy. The forensic vulnerabilities exposed by journalists on the ground in Afghanistan demonstrated the institutional hazards of public defense rhetoric and high visibility. In response, executive decision-makers transitioned the primary operational responsibility for remote targeted strikes away from transparent military structures toward classified intelligence operations, fundamentally altering the legal, technical, and accountability frameworks governing remote warfare.

| Operational Feature                     | February 2002 Strike (Afghanistan)                                                                  | November 3, 2002 Strike (Yemen)                                                                                   |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Operating Bureaucracy**               | U.S. Military (Department of Defense / U.S. Air Force).                                             | Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) Covert Operations.                                                              |
| **Public Visibility & Disclosure**      | High institutional visibility; public Pentagon press briefings and congressional testimony.         | Official silence; institutional classification; absolute refusal to confirm or deny operational involvement.      |
| **Post-Strike Justification Model**     | Active public defense of target legitimacy ("not innocents"; "appropriate target").                 | Covert legal validation inferred purely by outcome; classified executive authorization.                           |
| **Forensic Accountability & Challenge** | Strike exposed by ground journalists (_NYT_, _WashPost_); public repudiation by local eyewitnesses. | Closed operational loop; formally challenged by the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) and the United Nations. |

The structural contrast between the February 2002 strike in Afghanistan and the November 3, 2002 strike in Yemen illustrates this policy evolution. In the Yemen engagement, a CIA-operated Predator targeted a moving vehicle in Marib province, killing suspected Al Qaeda operative Qaed Salim Sinan al-Harethi alongside five other individuals—including U.S. citizen Ahmed Hijazi. Rather than mounting the aggressive public defense seen in February 2002, executive officials adopted strict classification and official silence.

Forensic verification was entirely replaced by outcome-based justification: the successful elimination of a confirmed HVT was leveraged off-the-record to implicitly validate the strike's legality and targeting precision. Secondary civilian casualties and the extrajudicial strike on an American citizen were subsumed within covert operational walls, triggering long-term legal challenges by the ACLU and the United Nations Human Rights Council regarding the extrajudicial nature of remote strike protocols.

#### Strategic Takeaways for Executive Decision-Makers

- **Mitigate Visual Confirmation Bias & Epistemic Opacity:** Remote video feeds must never be treated as self-evident intelligence facts. Physical profiles, height estimations, and geographic location cannot serve as sole indicators of hostile intent without rigorous, multi-source human intelligence (HUMINT) contextualization.
- **Mandate Independent Ground-Truth Verification:** Closed-loop military self-evaluations—such as the 101st Airborne's brief post-strike sweep—generate institutional confirmation bias. Intelligence verification protocols must mandate independent, physical forensic investigations to establish objective strike outcomes.
- **Reject Technocratic Hubris ("Different Lethality"):** Fusing real-time surveillance media with kinetic launch capabilities compresses the decision-making cycle, dramatically elevating the risk of hasty, faulty operational engagements. Technological integration does not inherently improve target identification accuracy.
- **Counter Institutional Discounting of Local Human Intelligence:** Systematically dismissing local eyewitness accounts, family testimonies, and indigenous intelligence as untrustworthy or financially motivated creates catastrophic intelligence blind spots, alienates critical local populations, and severely compromises strategic legitimacy.

The enduring lesson of the 2002 Predator case study is that technological integration and machinic sophistication can never substitute for political, ethical, and forensic rigor in target identification.

## **From Biplanes to "Robots in the Sky": The Evolution and Strategic Controversies of Early American Air Power** (1921–1960s) {#evolution-of-air-power}

### 1. Introduction: The Clash Between Air Power Visionaries and Military Orthodoxy

Following the conclusion of World War I, a fierce institutional debate erupted within the United States military regarding the fundamental nature of modern warfare. On one side stood radical air power visionaries who argued that the airplane had permanently transformed armed conflict, rendering traditional army land forces and surface navies secondary or even obsolete. On the other side stood traditional naval orthodoxy, whose leadership viewed aircraft primarily as auxiliary tools—valuable for spotter reconnaissance or as light extensions of field artillery—rather than independent combat platforms capable of deciding the outcome of wars.

For students of military history and curriculum design, understanding this foundational era of military aviation reveals a crucial pedagogical paradox: early air technologies were simultaneously celebrated as revolutionary instruments of vertical domination and feared as volatile, highly unreliable machines prone to frequent operational failure. While champions of aviation promised swift, calculated victory from the sky, early field experience demonstrated that biplanes and primitive remote-controlled platforms were severely constrained by engine breakdowns, crude targeting mechanisms, radio signal failures, and adverse weather.

**Core Concept: Air Power Theory** **Air Power Theory** is the strategic doctrine asserting that controlling the skies through aerial bombardment and military aviation is the decisive mechanism to achieve victory in modern warfare, bypassing traditional land and sea engagements to strike directly at an adversary's core infrastructure and military assets.

This intense friction between theoretical doctrine and military skepticism quickly shifted from academic debate into the public eye, setting the stage for the first high-profile, real-world demonstrations of American air power in 1921.

### 2. The 1921 Milestones: Bombing Battleships and Domestic Policing

The year 1921 served as a watershed moment for American aviation. Through two distinct public events, advocates of air power attempted to prove that military aircraft could project decisive physical force against both major foreign naval surface combatants and domestic civil unrest.

#### 2.1 The _Ostfriesland_ Bombing Trials (July 1921)

Brigadier General William "Billy" Mitchell, the outspoken deputy commander of the Army Air Service, launched a public crusade to demonstrate that heavily armored capital ships were entirely vulnerable to aerial attack. In July 1921, the U.S. military conducted "Plan B," a series of joint army-navy demonstration bombing runs off the Virginia Capes against captured German naval vessels, culminating in attacks on the static, heavily armored German battleship _Ostfriesland_.

The initial day of the trials exposed the severe mechanical and operational limits of early air power. On July 20, adverse weather, primitive bombsights, and light munitions produced minimal structural damage to the vessel, leading the _New York Times_ to run the critical headline _"Bombs Fail to Sink the Ostfriesland."_ However, on July 21, Mitchell's heavy bombers returned equipped with massive 2,000-pound TNT munitions. By dropping these heavy bombs near the ship's hull, the resulting underwater shockwaves breached the vessel, sinking the _Ostfriesland_. Media coverage reversed course immediately, proclaiming _"Sinking the Ostfriesland"_ and asserting that Mitchell's claims were no longer fanciful. Nevertheless, naval conservatives remained deeply skeptical, highlighting that the _Ostfriesland_ was an anchored, stationary target incapable of maneuvering or returning anti-aircraft fire.

#### 2.2 Domestic Air Power in Mingo County (August–September 1921)

Just weeks after the _Ostfriesland_ trials, air power was deployed on domestic soil during a major armed labor dispute in West Virginia, where over 5,000 unionized coal miners confronted coal operators and local deputy sheriffs. General Mitchell boasted publicly to the press that his Air Service could swiftly end the uprising by dropping tear gas canisters onto the miners.

Federal military intervention was legally enabled by lingering World War I emergency measures utilized by Secretary of War Newton Baker, who leveraged statutory authority from wartime National Guard federalization to bypass standard legal constraints and deploy federal forces domestically. This established a precedent wherein domestic labor strikes were treated by military planners as foreign-inspired, criminal threats. However, actual operations fell far short of Mitchell's grandiose rhetoric. Out of twenty-one Army Air Service biplanes dispatched to the region, only fourteen arrived due to severe weather and mechanical breakdowns. These federal planes performed strictly aerial reconnaissance and dropped copies of President Warren G. Harding's peace proclamation. The actual aerial bombardment during the conflict was carried out extralegally by private coal companies, who hired civilian pilots to drop improvised bombs packed with nails and scrap metal onto union positions—though these crude weapons largely failed to detonate or missed their marks.

The mixed and contested results of these 1921 events demonstrated that static, undefended targets provided inadequate real-world operational data, pushing naval researchers in the 1930s to engineer mobile, remote-controlled target aircraft to test fleet defenses under realistic conditions.

### 3. The 1930s "Project Drone": Creating the World's First Remote Targets

Initiated in 1936 under the direction of Commander Delmar Fahrney at the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL), "Project Drone" sought to solve a critical training deficit: providing naval anti-aircraft gunners with realistic, maneuvering targets. The initiative was authorized after Chief of Naval Operations Admiral William Standley observed British anti-aircraft gunnery trials using the "Queen Bee"—a radio-controlled target aircraft—during the 1935 Second Naval Conference in London.

To operate these systems, signals were transmitted via radio frequency from a ground station or accompanying control aircraft directly to an onboard receiver connected to a hydro-mechanical control unit inside the target airframe. This system actuated the aircraft's control surfaces without a pilot at the controls.

#### 3.1 Insect Names and "Drunken" Test Flights

Searching for an official codename that built upon the British "Queen Bee" designation, Commander Fahrney and his NRL research team reviewed several insect-inspired names. They selected **"DRONE"**—referring to the stingless, mindless male worker bee whose biological existence is entirely dedicated to a single, expendable task.

Early flight testing at the NRL using modified biplane trainers, such as the **NT DRONE**, encountered severe technical hurdles. During an early radio-controlled test flight with safety pilot Fred Wallace onboard to assume manual control in an emergency, crossed aileron wiring caused the biplane to execute violent, unpredictable maneuvers upon engaging the remote gear. Fahrney described the harrowing incident in official reports as the "astonishing evolutions" of a "drunken pilot." Anthropomorphic naming—such as labeling an out-of-control mechanical system a "drunken pilot"—served as an essential psychological coping mechanism for engineers and military personnel struggling to rationalize the frequent, chaotic control failures of early automated systems.

#### 3.2 Transitioning from "Solo" to "Nolo" (No Live Operator)

Once basic radio-control systems were refined, project engineers prepared to fly airframes completely devoid of human aircrew. Drawing an analogy to a human aviator's first unassisted flight (a "Solo"), the Navy coined the term **"Nolo"** (**No Live Operator**) to designate pilotless flights guided entirely by remote radio signals.

Despite the explicitly mechanical objective of Nolo operations, naval personnel routinely humanized the airframes. Official NRL technical documents characterized the NT DRONE as "ever faithful" and "loyal," noting that safety pilots viewed the airframe's ultimate retirement "reverently," as if mourning a human comrade. Operational Nolo flights remained technically precarious; during landing attempts, cutting the engine throttle caused the drone's nose to drop abruptly, tearing off the nose gear and causing the airframe to skid violently along the runway.

Satisfying basic Nolo flight safety parameters allowed the Navy to transition its testing apparatus out of Virginia laboratory settings and into live-fire, open-ocean fleet exercises off the coast of San Diego starting in 1938.

#### 3.3 Fleet Gunnery Trials on the USS _Ranger_ and USS _Utah_

Between 1938 and 1940, Project Drone conducted live-fire fleet exercises to evaluate the effectiveness of naval anti-aircraft batteries:

- **USS** _**Ranger**_ **(August 1938):** When a radio-controlled drone executed unexpected maneuvering passes over the aircraft carrier, the ship's anti-aircraft shell bursts lagged far behind the maneuvering target, recording zero hits.
- **USS** _**Utah**_ **(March 1939):** Anti-aircraft gunners expended over 2,000 rounds of ammunition (1,500 service rounds and 500 target rounds) against diving drones without registering a single hit.

These sobering results exposed a major structural flaw in naval doctrine. Historically, gunnery crews trained exclusively using **towed sleeve targets**—fabric cylinders pulled by manned aircraft along fixed, predictable, linear paths. When confronted with maneuvering, diving drones, the range-finders and mechanical range-keepers utilized by shipboard fire-control teams proved completely useless. Towed sleeves had generated false confidence in fleet readiness. While progressive champions like Rear Admiral Claude Bloch noted that traditional anti-aircraft methods lacked elasticity and Admiral W.D. Leahy warned of severe vulnerabilities, conservative ship commanders like Captain John S. McCain of the USS _Ranger_ and Commander Walter E. Brown of the USS _Utah_ dismissed the trial data, arguing that drones were too fragile, costly, and unrepresentative of actual combat to justify altering fleet defense concepts.

##### **Top Takeaways from 1930s Drone Testing**

- **Doctrinal Disruption:** Live-fire trials invalidated the Navy's core assumption that surface ships could easily destroy incoming aerial attacks, proving that towed sleeve targets had created a false sense of security.
- **Technical Instability:** Early unmanned operations suffered from continuous signal lag, crossed control wiring, and severe landing crashes caused by sudden changes in aerodynamic pitch.
- **Anthropomorphic Framing:** Personnel consistently assigned human psychological traits ("loyal," "faithful," "drunken") to mechanical systems as a conceptual bridge to handle erratic control failures.
- **Institutional Resistance:** Conservative naval leadership routinely discounted empirical target trial data to protect traditional surface-warfare doctrines and capital warship expenditures.

The theoretical vulnerabilities in fleet air defense exposed during the 1930s drone trials became a tragic reality during the surprise Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.

### 4. World War II and "Project Option": The Television-Guided Assault Drone

The entry of the United States into World War II catalyzed a pivot in drone development: rather than employing pilotless aircraft merely as target practice, military engineers sought to convert explosive-laden airframes into remote-controlled, television-guided assault weapons.

#### 4.1 Vladimir Zworykin, RCA, and the "Electric Eye"

As early as 1934, RCA engineer Vladimir Zworykin had submitted a visionary memorandum titled "Flying Torpedo with an Electric Eye," proposing a radio-controlled weapon guided by a compact television camera. In 1942, under the Navy's top-secret **Project Option**, Interstate Aircraft began manufacturing the **TDR-1** assault drone. Built largely out of non-strategic plywood subcontracted to the Wurlitzer Musical Instrument Company, the TDR-1 was equipped with a 2,000-pound bomb and a nose-mounted RCA television camera that transmitted a live video feed back to a screen inside an accompanying control aircraft.

The TDR-1 functioned through a dual-aircraft operational architecture. The assault drone, equipped with an RCA television camera, transmitted a live video signal back to a television monitor operated by a control pilot inside an accompanying TBM Avenger control plane. The control pilot observed the incoming video feed, manipulated a radio-control joystick to send directional commands back to the drone, and guided the explosive-laden airframe directly into the target.

```txt
[ TDR-1 Assault Drone w/ TV Camera ] ---> (Live TV Feed) ---> [ Control Aircraft Operator ]
                                     <--- (Radio Signals) ---
```

Military advocates, such as Captain Oscar Smith, explicitly likened these television-guided drones to Japanese suicide tactics. However, Zworykin's early framing and subsequent military rhetoric projected a racialized, subhuman image onto Japanese "Suicide Corps" pilots while simultaneously framing the American television drone as a demonstration of "technical superiority." American planners masked the lethal reality of suicide bombardment behind a veneer of technological ingenuity, claiming moral high ground by replacing the human pilot with an "electric eye."

As Navy Project Option struggled with low-performance plywood airframes and production delays through 1942 and 1943, the Joint Chiefs transferred excess RCA television control equipment to the Army Air Forces in mid-1944 to modify heavy bombers for European operations.

#### 4.2 Combat Operations in the Pacific and Europe

The assault drone saw operational combat testing in both main combat theaters:

- **The Pacific Theater (STAG-1):** Between September and October 1944, Special Task Air Group One (STAG-1) deployed 46 TDR-1 assault drones in the Solomon Islands. While initial strikes against the beached Japanese freighter _Yamazuki Maru_ provided clear video footage for proof-of-concept films, broader combat results were poor. Over half of the deployed drones failed due to mechanical breakdowns, anti-aircraft fire, or unreadable television static. In his memoir _American Kamikaze_, operator James Hall recalled his horror when a control system failure caused his assault drone to veer off course and crash directly into a red cross painted on the roof of a military hospital on Hospital Ridge.
- **The European Theater (Projects Aphrodite and Castor):** The U.S. Army Air Forces modified "war-weary" B-17 bombers packed with 12,000 pounds of high explosives. Human pilots were required to take off manually and steer the heavy bomber toward the English coast before parachuting out, after which a trailing control plane assumed guidance via television feed. These operations targeted heavily fortified German submarine pens at **Helgoland**. However, severe television picture degradation routinely led to catastrophic targeting errors, including the accidental bombing of **Dune Island**. The program suffered from high failure rates, severe video static, and tragic accidents—including a premature mid-air explosion over England that killed Navy pilot Joseph Kennedy and his co-pilot **Wilford Willy**.

The post-WWII cancellation of assault drone initiatives yielded to new cybernetic theories and urgent reconnaissance requirements during the opening decades of the Cold War.

### 5. Cold War "Unmanning": Cybernetics, Surveillance, and Political Risk

During the 1950s and 1960s, the strategic orientation of pilotless aviation transitioned from explosive assault platforms to high-speed, long-range aerial reconnaissance.

#### 5.1 "The Bee with an Electronic Brain"

In 1953, Ryan Aeronautical introduced the **Ryan Firebee (Q-2)**, a jet-powered target drone capable of near-sonic speeds. Company marketing adopted the newly fashionable language of cybernetics, derived from the 1943 theoretical work of Norbert Wiener, Arturo Rosenblueth, and Julian Bigelow on "purposeful behavior" driven by mechanical feedback loops.

Promotional literature described the Firebee as a "parasite" launched from a B-26 "mother" plane, guided by a "black box" and an "electronic brain." This corporate cybernetic rhetoric constructed a "machinelike monad," creating a public illusion that the Firebee flew with complete machine autonomy. In operational reality, the aircraft remained utterly dependent upon extensive human support networks, ground controllers, tracking radars, and remote radio pilots.

#### 5.2 The U-2 Crisis and the Shift to Unmanned Reconnaissance

On May 1, 1960, an American U-2 high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft piloted by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union. Powers' capture and subsequent public trial created a massive diplomatic humiliation for the Eisenhower administration.

In response, a classified 1961 Department of Defense report titled _"Alternative Reconnaissance Systems"_ formally redefined the strategic concept of **"unmanning."** Removing the pilot was no longer framed merely as an engineering accomplishment, but as an indispensable political instrument. By deploying pilotless aircraft for covert intelligence gathering over hostile territory, executive leadership could eliminate "political risk"—ensuring that if an airframe were shot down, there would be no captured pilot, no public trial, and no severe international fallout.

##### **3 Strategic Takeaways on the Concept of "Unmanning"**

1. **Political Risk Insulation:** "Unmanning" shifted the primary value of pilotless flight from preserving pilot lives to insulating the executive branch from diplomatic embarrassment and eliminating prisoner-of-war liabilities.
2. **Cybernetic Rhetoric vs. Human Reality:** Framing drones as autonomous "electronic brains" or self-contained "black boxes" reinforced Cold War "closed-world" ideology and national security anxiety, masking the complex human labor, tracking radar teams, and ground control infrastructures behind every flight.
3. **Objective Fact-Making:** Integrating cameras onto pilotless platforms re-engineered aerial reconnaissance into "hard facts," utilizing vertical imagery to legitimize state intelligence decisions under the protective cover of neutral, machine-generated objectivity.
