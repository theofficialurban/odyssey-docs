### 3. Spiritual Transformation and the Call to Preach

In the fall of 1823, William Arthur established his household in Burlington, Vermont, gaining employment as a copyist in a lawyer's office to pursue legal studies. However, his professional path altered dramatically following a profound religious conversion on the shores of Lake Champlain.

While living in Burlington, William attended an outdoor Baptist gathering one winter Sunday simply out of curiosity, observing the congregation break a hole in the thick ice of Lake Champlain to perform immersion baptisms. The solemnity and piety of the rite deeply moved him, prompting a complete religious awakening. He publicly professed his faith and was baptized, initially aligning with the Free-will Baptists in Berkshire before uniting with the Close Communion (Regular) Baptists after finding that their doctrines better matched his theological convictions.

Recognizing his natural gifts for public speaking, his brethren encouraged him to preach. William quickly gained renown for his sharp intellect, distinct articulation, apt figures of speech, and biting wit when defending his beliefs. During an early lecture at a schoolhouse in East Berkshire, an elderly local figure named Squire Roice—father of future Vermont Supreme Court Judge Stephen Roice—attempted to publicly rattle the young preacher before the service by demanding, _"How many islands are there in the Grecian Archipelago?"_ William looked him in the eye and cleverly turned the Squire's Masonic background against him, replying that _"there are as many islands in the Grecian Archipelago as there were buttons on Moses' britches."_ Amused and impressed by the swift response, the Squire commended his sharp mind and became a supportive parishioner.

Arthur's early ministry was also defined by forceful theological defense. While pastoring in Fairfield, he engaged in a legendary four-hour pulpit debate with the local Congregationalist minister, Rev. Benjamin Wooster, fiercely arguing the biblical doctrine of adult immersion versus infant sprinkling.

"His articulation was distinct, his figures apt, their application easy, not unfrequently manifesting the keenest wit. His sarcasms, when used, were cutting... His taste was nice and delicate; and would read men's characters accurately by their physiognomy. His conversation was earnest, lively, and always listened to with attention." — **Rev. Alvah Sabin**, _Contemporary and Fellow Minister_

With his newly recognized ministerial license and formidable oratorical gifts, Rev. Arthur embarked on an extensive itinerant ministry throughout the frontier towns of Vermont and New York.

### 4. Itinerant Ministry and Frontier Life Across Vermont and New York

Rev. William Arthur’s pastoral career spanned several decades, characterized by frequent moves, severe economic trials, academic leadership, and authorial achievements across rural pulpits and growing urban centers.

2. **Jericho & Richford, Vermont (1825–1827):** Moved his family to Jericho in late 1825, welcoming his third daughter, Almeda, in January 1826, before relocating to Richford on the Canadian border in the spring of 1826.
3. **Waterville & North Fairfield, Vermont (1827–1832):** Relocated to Waterville in 1827 and moved to North Fairfield in May 1828, where he was formally ordained into the Baptist ministry around 1828. Frontier life presented extreme adversity here; during one harsh mid-winter storm, Rev. Alvah Sabin visited Arthur's small household to find the crippled minister completely out of bread and firewood, near despair, before a supportive meeting renewed his sense of calling. In November 1830, a son named Chester Abell Arthur (named for family physician Dr. Chester Abell) was born in Fairfield, though the child tragically died in Burlington in the summer of 1831. Financial necessity led Arthur to teach winter school terms in Stanbridge, Canada, from 1830 to 1832.
4. **Williston, Vermont (1832–1833):** Assumed the pastorate of a newly organized Baptist church and established a private academy in a building owned by Rev. Peter Chase. The long trajectory of Rev. Arthur's life—from a crippled Irish immigrant boy to an esteemed, degree-holding author and divine—offers a vivid window into the resilient spirit of 19th-century frontier ministry.

## **Investigative Report: Patronage, Systemic Coercion, and Electoral Fraud in the New York Custom House under Chester A. Arthur** {#investigative-primer}

### 1. Executive Overview & Political Context (1869–1878)

During the post-Civil War decade, the New York Custom House functioned as the institutional nerve center of American political patronage, party finance, and machine governance. Collecting the vast majority of the nation’s customs revenue, the Custom House commanded an immense workforce and substantial financial resources, which factional leaders systematically converted into political power. Controlling this operational hub enabled political bosses to enforce party discipline, extract political assessments from federal employees, and deploy government personnel to capture local ward associations. By dominating these primary organizations, machine leaders manipulated primary elections, handpicked convention delegates, and maintained complete factional hegemony over both local and state political apparatuses.

Chester A. Arthur’s elevation within this network began well before his appointment to federal office, rooted in cross-party administrative bargains. In 1869, Arthur secured the lucrative position of Counsel to the Commissioners of the Board of Taxes and Assessments in New York City, a post carrying a commanding salary of $10,000 per annum. This appointment was established through structural collusion between Republican leadership and Democratic "Boss" William M. Tweed. To circumvent statutory limitations—specifically legislative prohibitions restricting the Common Council from creating new municipal offices or raising salaries—the Tammany-dominated state legislature engineered a legislative rider at the end of Section 2 of Chapter 898 of the Session Laws of 1869 (passed May 17, 1869, Volume 2, pages 2264–2265, entitled "An act in relation to taxes, &c."). This statutory maneuver transferred salary-fixing authority directly to the County Board of Supervisors, authorizing the Tax Commissioners to employ counsel at a compensation determined by the board.

On July 5, 1869, Tax Commissioners Secretary E.F. Babcock formally notified the Board of Supervisors of Arthur's appointment (clericating the statutory citation in official correspondence as "Chapter 898 of the Laws of 1879"). The Board of Supervisors—consisting of Walter Roache (President), Henry Smith, William M. Tweed, Andreas Willmann, John Fox, James Hayes, Andrew Bleakley, Isaac J. Oliver, Gerson N. Herrman, Orison Blunt, and John Brice—immediately took up the compensation resolution. While Tweed structurally directed the municipal board that created the position, the official recorded roll call vote adopting the $10,000 salary resolution was passed by an eight-voter affirmative majority consisting of Supervisors Bleakley, Blunt, Brice, Fox, Hays, Herrman, Board President Walter Roache, and Smith. Holding this $10,000 municipal tax counsel post under the Democratic Tammany Ring for three years created a profound structural conflict of interest, as Arthur cultivated lucrative political ties with Tweed while simultaneously preparing to assume control over federal revenue administration.

Arthur’s mastery of municipal patronage and inter-party maneuvering paved the way for his presidential appointment as Collector of the Port of New York on November 20, 1871, succeeding Thomas Murphy and placing the vast institutional apparatus of the Custom House directly under his command.

### 2. Institutional Patronage and Systemic Coercion: The "Slaughter" of Intra-Party Dissent

Under Collector Chester A. Arthur, civil service patronage was systematically weaponized to enforce uncompromising factional loyalty within the Republican Party. The Custom House operated not as an impartial administrative body, but as an arm of machine discipline where federal job security was tied directly to political obedience. Controlling primary elections at the district level was vital to machine survival; any independent movement or non-officeholding faction that threatened the machine's authority faced immediate administrative retaliation and punitive purges.

A documented case of this political coercion occurred during the 1874 Assembly campaign in the 21st Assembly District. William Haw, Jr., a former President of the 21st Assembly District Republican Association and an Inspector of Stations in the New York Post Office, was unanimously selected as an Assembly candidate by a district convention composed of 42 non-officeholding citizens. Seeking to force Haw's withdrawal in favor of an un-nominated candidate backed by the State Committee operating out of the Fifth Avenue Hotel (headed by Surveyor George H. Sharpe), Collector Arthur summoned Haw to his office. When Haw refused to forfeit his independent nomination, Collector Arthur issued an explicit administrative ultimatum, threatening that if Haw did not withdraw, Arthur would "slaughter him, slaughter his friends, and slaughter his boy."

When Haw defied the order, Arthur executed the threat: Haw’s son was summarily dismissed from his position in the Custom House, and Arthur requested Postmaster Thomas L. James to discharge Haw himself from the Post Office on November 10, 1874, explicitly citing Haw's failure to obey Arthur's political orders in the district.

### 3. Tactical Analysis of Primary Election Fraud

In the 19th-century political machine model, primary elections served as the vital gateway to institutional control. By controlling the primary elections of local assembly district associations, machine bosses dictated the selection of convention delegates. These compliant delegates were then dispatched to state and national nominating conventions, ensuring the machine's chosen candidates secured nominations and perpetuating federal patronage networks.

A primary example of electoral manipulation occurred during the September 3, 1875 primary in Harlem's 21st Assembly District, held to select delegates to the Saratoga State Convention. To defeat the independent Republican ticket, Collector Arthur, Surveyor Sharpe, and Colonel George Bliss organized the mass importation of off-district federal officeholders. Federal employees from across New York City were transported into Harlem using revenue cutters, tugboats, steamships, and railroad cars. Once at the polling place, these non-resident federal employees engaged in systematic voter impersonation, voting between 10 and 30 times each under the registered names of legitimate district association members. When local leaders like William Haw, Jr. detected the repeaters and attempted to challenge their illegal votes, United States Appraiser A.P. Ketchum actively encouraged the repeaters to keep voting. Ketchum then summoned local police and had Haw arrested and detained at the station house for half an hour on charges of interfering with the election, allowing the illegal voting to proceed unchallenged until Haw's release.

The machine expanded its tactical repertoire during the March 17, 1876 Primary Election for State Convention delegates through physical ballot manipulation and tissue ballot fraud. Non-resident voters and machine operatives deposited specially manufactured pink tissue paper tickets measuring approximately half an inch wide by two inches long. Operatives tightly rolled 20 to 25 of these thin tissue ballots inside a single outer ballot before dropping them into the vote box. When election officials aggregated the votes, these bundled ballots expanded upon opening, artificially multiplying the tally for machine delegates.

To ensure these fraudulent counts were certified, the machine subverted the local Board of Inspectors. Custom House officials and former Tammany political operatives—including a plumber formerly employed under Democratic Boss William M. Tweed—were placed as primary election judges. These inspectors suppressed voter challenges, refused to let non-machine members object to obvious fraud, counted machine tickets while lumping opposition votes together, and validated the corrupt tallies.

These field operations in electoral manipulation were mirrored by widespread administrative irregularities and financial exploitation within the Custom House administrative hierarchy.

### 4. Administrative Irregularities, Favors, and Financial Exploitation

Without meritocratic oversight or civil service accountability, high-ranking Custom House officials routinely diverted federal property, executive authority, and public payrolls for private gain and factional favors. Public assets were frequently treated as personal commodities to accommodate political allies and high-ranking administration figures.

Sworn testimony provided by former Customs Inspector Payson Dwight revealed the unauthorized use of federal maritime assets and executive privileges for non-official purposes. Dwight testified that in late September or October 1874, Surveyor George H. Sharpe telegraphed the Barge Officer to hold the _Manhattan_, a government revenue cutter, subject to his personal orders. Collector Arthur and Surveyor Sharpe then boarded the revenue cutter and intercepted the incoming steamship _Russia_ past sunset in the stream at quarantine. Arthur and Sharpe boarded the _Russia_ to escort Mrs. Belknap (wife of the Secretary of War) and Mrs. Marsh, who were arriving with extensive personal baggage. By order of Sharpe, approximately 43 unexamined trunks and parcels belonging to Mrs. Belknap were bypassed through standard customs clearance protocols and transferred directly off the vessel in the stream. Sharpe subsequently issued a written order directing that 13 trunks held in the vessel's hold be released without inspection to Colonel George Bliss for delivery to the Fifth Avenue Hotel.

Administrative corruption also infected federal payrolls through ghost employment and the misappropriation of public funds. Testimony from Captain Benjamin Page—former Assistant Collector of Internal Revenue under General A.P. Ketchum—exposed fraudulent payroll practices. Page documented that during Ketchum's tenure as Collector of Internal Revenue (prior to Ketchum's appointment as United States Appraiser), a colored man-servant named Robert Jones was carried continuously on the official Internal Revenue payroll at an annual salary of $1,500. In reality, Jones performed no official duties for the revenue service; instead, his salary was drawn entirely for domestic service as a private servant in Ketchum's home.

These systemic abuses, political purges, and payroll frauds eventually triggered a formal investigation by the Federal Commission, leading to public exposure and political consequences.

### 5. Evidentiary Synthesis: The 1877 Custom House Investigation and Historical Impact

The launch of the Customs Investigating Commission in 1877 marked a major turning point in exposing executive abuse and patronage corruption during the Gilded Age. The Commission gathered extensive testimony regarding administrative misconduct, election fraud, and political coercion within the New York Custom House.

On May 21, 1877, a delegation of Republican citizens from the 21st Assembly District appeared before the Investigating Commission to deliver testimony on machine operations, as reported in _The Reporter and Harlem Local_ on May 26, 1877:

- **John E. Hegarty:** Testified regarding his summary termination on July 18, 1875, from his position as opener and packer in the Appraiser’s Depot. Hegarty presented letters of competency from his superior officers and proof of four years of honorable Union Army service, confirming that Superintendent Lawrence Harney, General Appraiser A.P. Ketchum, and Surveyor Sharpe demanded his discharge solely because he refused to support the "Custom House party" against independent Republican William Haw, Jr.
- **James O. Roper:** Exposed a complete chain of corrupt solicitation and conditional job offers within the Custom House. Roper testified that in 1875, Custom House Inspectors Stephen S. Doty and William Starrett offered to secure him a federal appointment if he used his influence to make Henry Welch chairman of the primary Board of Inspectors. To confirm the offer, Roper met with Surveyor Sharpe—who promised that Collector Arthur would handle the appointment—and General Appraiser A.P. Ketchum, who provided a written letter addressed to Arthur's secretary, Mr. Phillipps. When Roper ultimately refused to vote for machine nominee Welch for primary chairman, his promised Custom House appointment was withheld.
- **Payson Dwight:** Detailed administrative irregularities, including the improper use of federal vessels and customs evasions for political figures. Dwight documented how the revenue cutter _Manhattan_ was deployed to board the steamship _Russia_ past sunset, bypassing normal border clearance for 43 trunks and packages belonging to the wife of Secretary Belknap, alongside additional written orders releasing hold trunks directly to Colonel George Bliss.
- **William Haw, Jr.:** Delivered testimony detailing the machine's coercive practices, including Arthur's direct threat to "slaughter" Haw, his friends, and his son. Haw provided a comprehensive breakdown of election fraud during the 1875 and 1876 primaries, documenting the transportation of non-resident repeaters via tugboats, steamships, and railroad cars, his own police detainment organized by Appraiser Ketchum, the deployment of pink tissue ballots to subvert the vote, and his subsequent retaliatory discharge from the Post Office under Postmaster Thomas L. James.

The Commission's findings resulted in significant institutional and political repercussions. Following the audit of the Custom House's operations, President Rutherford B. Hayes removed Chester A. Arthur from his post as Collector of the Port of New York. The investigation highlighted the vast financial rewards generated by Gilded Age patronage systems; despite entering the office as a poor man, Arthur accumulated an estimated personal fortune of nearly $3,000,000 during his tenure as Collector, establishing him as a major political power broker.

Arthur's career demonstrates a striking historical irony. Despite his documented record of electoral manipulation, political purges, and machine corruption, Arthur was nominated for the Vice-Presidency at the 1880 Republican National Convention in Chicago—a political selection engineered by New York's senior Senator to balance the national ticket. Following his nomination and election, Arthur acceded to the Vice-Presidency and ultimately to the Presidency of the United States. The primary records and sworn testimonies compiled during the 1877 investigation remain invaluable resources for political historians and civil service integrity auditors, offering an evidentiary record of the mechanisms of Gilded Age political corruption, executive overreach, and spoils-system governance.

### 1. Constitutional Framework and the Legal Nature of the Challenge

#### 1.1 Legal Standard under Article II and the 12th Amendment

The foundational stability of the American executive branch depends entirely upon strict adherence to the jurisdictional prerequisites established by the Framers in the United States Constitution. Under Article II, Section 1, Clause 5, the Constitution establishes an absolute threshold: "No person except a natural born citizen... shall be eligible to the office of President." This mandate is reinforced by Subdivision 3 of the Twelfth Amendment, which extends this jurisdictional requirement directly to the Vice-Presidency: "But no person constitutionally ineligible to the office of President shall be eligible to that of Vice-President of the United States." Far from being mere formal technicalities, these legal provisions serve as non-negotiable structural safeguards designed to protect the executive branch from foreign influence and foreign allegiance. When a candidate's territorial nativity is shrouded in ambiguity or active deception, the challenge strikes directly at the core of constitutional legitimacy and executive jurisdiction.

In evaluating candidates with complex cross-border familial histories, the legal interpretation of "natural-born citizen" must be applied with uncompromising precision. As formally articulated by United States Senator Thomas F. Bayard in his authoritative legal opinion dated January 10, 1881, the term "natural-born citizen," as used within Article II and federal statutes, is strictly defined as "a native of the U.S." While statutory provisions allow that the legal naturalization of an alien father before his minor child turns twenty-one confers derivative citizenship upon that child, such statutory naturalization does not satisfy the organic requirement of native territorial birth mandated for the presidency. Derivative citizenship or subsequent naturalization cannot substitute for physical birth within the sovereign borders of the United States.

#### 1.2 Antecedent Doubts and Pre-Acceptance Investigations

Upon receiving the Republican nomination for Vice President at the Chicago Convention in the summer of 1880, Chester A. Arthur faced an immediate political and legal crisis regarding his birthplace. When party managers pressed him to designate the specific municipality of his nativity prior to drafting his formal letter of acceptance, Arthur exhibited an extraordinary inability to state his precise birthplace. This failure was not a mere lapse of memory, but reflected a deliberate attempt to avoid public record scrutiny while he privately verified whether any documentary evidence of his Canadian birth remained accessible in foreign archives.

To navigate this constitutional vulnerability, Arthur executed a series of calculated evasions designed to evaluate his exposure before committing to a public claim:

- **Public Announcement of Recreational Excursion:** Arthur publicly announced to the national press that he was departing on a recreational "fishing excursion" to Canada with his associate, Robert G. Dun of the Mercantile Agency, ostensibly to secure quiet surroundings to draft his formal acceptance letter.
- **Covert Archival Search in Montreal:** Under cover of this fishing trip, Arthur traveled directly with Dun to Montreal, Province of Quebec, Canada, where he personally initiated a clandestine search of provincial and civil archives to determine if official documentation of his birth on Canadian soil was publicly preserved.
- **Assessment of Negative Record Findings:** Upon determining that Canadian authorities had not retained a formal civil registration of his birth in the provincial records, Arthur concluded he could assert an American birth without immediate fear of official documentary refutation.
- **Co-opting of Deceased Brother's Birthplace:** Arthur selected North Fairfield, Franklin County, Vermont—the established birthplace of a deceased younger brother—as his own claimed birth locality.
- **Execution of Political Acceptance:** Having established this plausible domestic venue, Arthur issued his public letter of acceptance, claiming native-born American status through an appropriated domestic location.

Arthur's evasive actions and covert archival maneuvers in 1880 demonstrate the necessity of a rigorous genealogical investigation into the patriarchal antecedents and cross-border movements of his father, the Reverend William Arthur.

### 2. Genealogical Trajectory and Cross-Border Movements of the Arthur Family

In 1796, Gavin Arthur had a son, named William Arthur in honor of King William III (William of Orange). William received a common school education at the Blue School in Belfast. In 1818, upon completing his schooling, he departed Derry, Ireland, aboard a sailing vessel, landing at Three Rivers, Lower Canada (Quebec).

### 3. The Evidentiary Discrepancy: Dual-Son Hypothesis and Identity Substitution

#### 3.1 Witness Testimony on the Birth of the First Son in Dunham, Canada (1828)

The primary evidentiary conflict disproving Chester A. Arthur's natural-born citizenship claim centers on the birth of his family's first male child on British soil in the spring of 1828. During this period, while William Arthur was transitioning between pastoral charges in Waterville and North Fairfield, Vermont, Mrs. Malvina Arthur returned to her parents' home (George Washington Stone) at Dunham Flats, Quebec, for her confinement. Multiple independent witnesses documented this birth:

- **Mrs. Stevens (Dunham Flats, Quebec):** Aged 80 at the time of her deposition; childhood playmate and lifelong friend of Malvina Stone. She swore under oath that Mrs. Arthur visited her parents' home in Dunham Flats frequently and explicitly recalled delivering and making the first clothes for Mrs. Arthur’s eldest boy, born in Dunham on **March 16 or 18, 1828**. She recounted that the baby's grandfather joked that because the infant was half-Irish and born a day before or after St. Patrick's Day (March 17), it was fortunate he escaped being named "St. Patrick." She further recalled William Arthur jokingly suggesting the name "Tommy Wood" (after an old suitor of Mrs. Arthur), before Mrs. Stevens and Mrs. Arthur officially named the infant **William Chester Alan Arthur**. She attested that the child was the physical image of his father.
- **L. I. H. Corey (Stanbridge, Quebec):** Resident of Stanbridge who personally knew the Arthur family, corroborating the birth of William Arthur's firstborn son at Dunham Flats, Quebec, prior to the family's permanent settlement in Vermont.
- **Mrs. Captain John Chandler (Stanbridge, Quebec):** Neighbor to George Washington Stone at Meggs Corners, Dunham. She remembered attending school in 1830 with the eldest Arthur daughter, Regina, who shared a letter detailing her siblings' multi-part names, specifically identifying her eldest brother as **William Abell Chester Arthur** (or William Chester Alan Arthur), noting that all Arthur children were assigned three to four given names.

Concurrently, a second male child was born to the family on American soil two years later, setting the stage for a critical divergence in the family record.

#### 3.2 The Birth and Death of Chester Abell Arthur (1830–1831)

In November 1830, while William Arthur was employed as a schoolmaster in Stanbridge, Quebec (returning to Fairfield periodically to preach), Mrs. Malvina Arthur gave birth to a second male child in North Fairfield, Vermont. This child was named **Chester Abell Arthur** in honor of Dr. Chester Abell, the attending family physician and close companion of William Arthur.

To establish the evidentiary weight of the records surrounding this second child, witness testimonies are divided into direct firsthand recollections and secondary community accounts:

##### Direct Firsthand Observations

- **M. H. Bliss (Swanton, VT):** Boarded at Rev. William Arthur's home in Fairfield during the winter of 1829–1830. He testified that he personally rocked the infant **Chester Abell Arthur** in his cradle, noting the child was named explicitly for the attending family physician.
- **Calvin Abell (Sheldon, VT):** Brother of Dr. Chester Abell. He testified under oath that his brother attended Mrs. Arthur in her confinement and was permitted by the parents to name the newborn child **Chester Abell Arthur**.
- **Ezra Wright Sherman (Montgomery Centre, VT):** Attended school under William Arthur in Fairfield. He testified that in November 1830, a male child was born in Fairfield and named after Dr. Chester Abell. He confirmed traveling with William Arthur to Dunham in November 1830 and recalled that the family had two daughters and one infant son at home.
- **Leonard D. Geer (Fairfield, VT):** Aged 69; born in Fairfield. He deposed under oath that William Arthur married in Berkshire, moved into Canada where several children were born, and subsequently came to Fairfield. Geer explicitly swore: _"There was one born named Chester Abell Arthur. None by the name of Chester Alan Arthur was born here... no Chester Alan Arthur was born here in 1830."_
- **J. H. Corey & "Uncle Jack" Baker (Stanbridge, Quebec):** Confirmed that during the summer of 1831, while Mrs. Arthur was visiting friends near Burlington, Vermont, her infant boy, Chester Abell Arthur, fell severely ill and died. Baker and Corey recalled Elder Arthur lamenting in Stanbridge that he was so impoverished at the time that he lacked the funds necessary to bury the child.

##### Secondary / Hearsay Recollections

- **Salmon Soule (Fairfield, VT):** Aged 85; testified that he personally knew Elder William Arthur during his Fairfield pastorate (1827–1831) and recalled _"hearing at that time"_ of a boy born and named after Dr. Chester Abell (the physician who had amputated Soule's toe in 1826).

Physical evidence corroborates the real-world presence and death of the attending physician in Fairfield. In the cemetery at North Fairfield, Vermont, a tombstone stands with the following inscription:

**CEMETERY INSCRIPTION: NORTH FAIRFIELD, VERMONT** _Sacred to the Memory of Dr. Chester Abell, who died June 2d, A.D. 1832, Aged 36 years._

Furthermore, local reports collected by investigator A. P. Hinman and recorded in correspondence from Lindal Corey (January 1881) document a persistent local belief surrounding the infant's death. Informants, including Joseph Baker, noted that Elder Arthur, facing destitute circumstances in Burlington during the summer of 1831, was rumored to have surrendered his deceased infant's remains to local medical practitioners for dissection to relieve his pressing financial liabilities.

The premature death of Chester Abell Arthur in 1831 set the stage for a calculated identity substitution designed to preserve political eligibility.

#### 3.3 Synthesis of Witness Depositions and the Alleged Name Modification

The evidence compiled by investigator A. P. Hinman and supported by local informants (including Lindal Corey) points to an identity substitution executed to obscure foreign nativity. Following the death of the infant Chester Abell Arthur in Burlington in 1831, the family retained a single surviving son: the Canadian-born **William Chester Alan Arthur** (born March 1828 in Dunham, Quebec).

When the family moved to Hinesburgh, Vermont, in 1834, Mrs. Arthur gave birth to another male child, who was formally named **William Arthur Jr.** (later Major and Paymaster, U.S. Army). To resolve the confusion of having two living sons bearing the prefix "William," the family dropped "William" from the elder son's name.

### 4. Forensic Analysis of Public Record Gaps and Institutional Documents

#### 4.1 Official Searches and Absence of Civil Birth Records in Vermont

To evaluate Chester A. Arthur’s eligibility claims, formal forensic searches of municipal, county, and state archives were conducted across Vermont. These searches revealed a total absence of contemporary birth registrations for any child born to William and Malvina Arthur within the state between 1825 and 1835.

Key official findings include:

- **Chittenden County Certification (Feb. 1, 1881):** County Clerk A. J. Howard formally certified that no records of births, marriages, or deaths for the children of William and Malvina Arthur existed within Chittenden County archives between January 1, 1822, and January 1, 1841.
- **Waterville Town Certificate (Feb. 2, 1881):** Town Clerk E. H. Shattuck certified an exhaustive search of Waterville town records, finding no registration for any Arthur family births or deaths.
- **Fairfield Town Certificate (Dec. 31, 1880):** Town Clerk A. G. Soule issued a sworn certification that a search of Fairfield birth records between 1825 and 1835 yielded no record for a child named Chester A. Arthur, Chester Alan Arthur, or William Chester Alan Arthur.
- **Vermont State Archives Certificate (Jan. 28, 1881):** Deputy Secretary of State C. W. Porter issued a state-level certification attesting that the official records of the Secretary of State contained no birth or death entries for the Arthur family from 1822 through 1841.

The investigation in Fairfield encountered notable partisan resistance. As detailed in correspondence from attorney W. H. Fairchild dated January 3, 1881, Fairfield Town Clerk A. G. Soule—a committed partisan Republican—objected to inspecting the records in an official capacity. Soule delayed issuing his negative search certificate for weeks until forced to comply by legal demands.

Despite the complete absence of civil birth registrations, Chester A. Arthur successfully navigated institutional admissions by making flexible age declarations.

### 5. Political Patronage, Custom House Irregularities, and Credibility Context

#### 5.1 The Tweed Ring Appointment and Custom House Patronage System

Evaluating Chester A. Arthur's credibility regarding his citizenship claims requires examining his record of political maneuvering, patronage distribution, and administrative practices in New York City. His political rise was defined by alliances and partisan operations:

- **Tweed Ring Alliance & Tax Commission Counsel (1869):** In 1869, Democratic Boss William M. Tweed used his political influence to secure a position for Arthur, a Republican operative. Tweed instructed State Senator Thomas Creamer to attach a legislative rider to Chapter 898 of the Laws of 1869 (passed May 17, 1869). This rider authorized the Commissioners of Taxes and Assessments to employ legal counsel at a salary fixed by the Tweed-controlled Board of Supervisors. On July 5, 1869, Arthur was appointed Counsel at a salary of **$10,000 per year**—a post created despite legal prohibitions against creating new city offices. Arthur held this position for three years, resigning only when Tammany Hall attempted to force Republican officeholders to actively conceal ring robberies.
- **Custom House Misconduct & Revenue Cutter Misuse:** Succeeding Thomas Murphy as Collector of the Port of New York on November 20, 1871, Arthur converted the Custom House into a political machine. In September or October 1874, Assistant Boarding Officer Payson Dwight testified that Collector Arthur and Surveyor George H. Sharpe commandeered the United States Revenue Cutter _Manhattan_ to meet the steamship _Russia_ after sunset. They bypassed standard customs inspection to retrieve 43 trunks and packages belonging to Mrs. Secretary Belknap and Mrs. Marsh, transferring the uninspected baggage directly to the Fifth Avenue Hotel.
- **Primary Election Fraud ("Tissue Ballots"):** During the 21st Assembly District primary election on March 17, 1876, Arthur’s custom house faction deployed Federal employees to manipulate elections using "tissue ballots"—thin tickets half an inch wide and two inches long—allowing repeaters to deposit 20 to 25 folded ballots at a time. Repeaters were transported into the district via tugboats, railroad cars, and steamboats under Arthur's political management. When Republican candidate William Haw, Jr. attempted to stop repeaters at the polls, Appraiser General A. P. Ketchum had Haw falsely arrested by police to enable the repeaters to vote. Testimony further revealed that Ketchum maintained a man-servant, Robert Jones, on the federal payroll as a ghost employee at a salary of $1,500 per year without performing official duties.
- **Coercion and Political "Slaughter" of Officeholders:** Sworn testimony before the 1877 Custom House Investigating Commission revealed systematic political coercion under Arthur's direction:

##### **INVESTIGATIVE TESTIMONY: 1877 CUSTOM HOUSE COMMISSION**

- **William Haw, Jr. (Inspector of Stations, Post Office):** Testified that when he refused Arthur's order to withdraw his candidacy in the 21st Assembly District, Collector Arthur explicitly threatened to _"slaughter him, slaughter his friends, and slaughter his boy."_ Haw's son was fired from the Custom House, and Haw was removed from his Post Office position on Arthur's direct demand.
- **John E. Hegarty (Opener & Packer, Appraiser's Dept.):** Testified he was arbitrarily discharged on July 18, 1875, despite four years of honorable Union Army service, because Surveyor Sharpe and Collector Arthur demanded his removal for refusing to support the Custom House political faction against William Haw, Jr.
- **James O. Roper (Advertising Agent):** Testified that Custom House officials explicitly offered him a Federal appointment in exchange for delivering political control of the local Board of Inspectors to Arthur's favored candidate.
- **Payson Dwight (Inspector of Customs):** Testified to widespread corruption, coercion, and political intimidation enforced systematically throughout the Custom House staff under Arthur's tenure.

Following the 1877 commission investigation, President Rutherford B. Hayes removed Arthur from his post as Collector. However, Arthur retired with an estimated $3,000,000 accumulated during his political tenure.

This record of political maneuvering provides the forensic context for evaluating Arthur's willingness to conceal his constitutional inadmissibility when nominated for the vice-presidency in 1880.

#### 5.2 Evidentiary Implications for Public Integrity and Constitutional Compliance

The accumulated evidence gathered across legal, genealogical, and forensic records provides a compelling case regarding Chester A. Arthur’s constitutional inadmissibility. Five primary evidentiary pillars disprove his claim of natural-born citizenship:

1. **Verified Canadian Birth of Firstborn Son:** Sworn depositions from direct witnesses (Mrs. Stevens, L. I. H. Corey, Mrs. Captain John Chandler) confirm that William Arthur’s eldest son, William Chester Alan Arthur, was born on British soil at Dunham Flats, Quebec, in March 1828.
